📘 Chapter 1

Introduction to Industrial Biotechnology

Industrial biotechnology applies biological systems — primarily microorganisms and their enzymes — to manufacture products of commercial value at scale.

Biotechnology is the application of living organisms, biological systems, or biological processes to develop products and technologies that benefit humanity. When applied at an industrial or manufacturing scale, it is called Industrial Biotechnology (also known as White Biotechnology).

Unlike medical biotechnology (Red) or agricultural biotechnology (Green), industrial biotechnology focuses on the use of microorganisms, cells, and enzymes to produce chemicals, fuels, food, textiles, and other valuable materials more sustainably and economically than conventional chemical processes.

Definition

📌 Industrial Biotechnology — Defined

Industrial Biotechnology is the application of biological organisms, systems, or processes in industrial production to convert raw biological materials (biomass, sugars, starch) into valuable products such as fuels, chemicals, enzymes, food additives, and pharmaceuticals through processes like fermentation.

Scope & Branches
Fermentation
🧫

Microbial Fermentation

Use of yeast, bacteria, and fungi to produce ethanol, organic acids, vitamins, and antibiotics.

Enzyme Tech
⚗️

Enzyme Technology

Isolation and application of enzymes (amylase, lipase, protease) for industrial processes.

Biofuels
⛽

Biofuel Production

Conversion of biomass into bioethanol, biodiesel, and biogas as renewable energy sources.

Bioprocessing
🏭

Bioprocess Engineering

Design and optimisation of bioreactors, upstream & downstream processing for scale-up production.

Why Industrial Biotechnology?

🌿 Advantages Over Conventional Methods

  • Uses renewable raw materials (sugars, starch, biomass) instead of fossil fuels
  • Operates under mild conditions (low temperature, ambient pressure) — energy efficient
  • Produces fewer toxic by-products — environmentally friendly
  • Microorganisms can be genetically improved to enhance yield
  • Continuous production possible in bioreactors
  • Wide range of products: antibiotics, alcohols, organic acids, vitamins, enzymes

Basic Concepts of Fermentation

Fermentation is the microbial transformation of organic compounds — typically sugars — into simpler products like alcohol, acids, and gases under controlled conditions.

Principle of Fermentation

The fundamental principle of fermentation is anaerobic catabolism of organic compounds. In the absence of sufficient oxygen, microorganisms cannot carry out full aerobic respiration. Instead, they partially oxidise glucose through glycolysis to produce pyruvate, which is then reduced to various end-products (ethanol, lactic acid, etc.) while regenerating NAD⁺ to allow glycolysis to continue.

Overall Equation — Alcoholic Fermentation
C₆H₁₂O₆ (Glucose) → 2 C₂H₅OH (Ethanol) + 2 CO₂ + 2 ATP
Catalysed by Zymase enzyme complex in Saccharomyces cerevisiae
Role of Microorganisms

Microorganisms are the biological catalysts of fermentation. They possess the specific enzymatic machinery to break down complex substrates into simpler products. Different microorganisms produce different end-products from the same starting material.

🍺

Yeast (Fungi)

Saccharomyces cerevisiae produces ethanol + CO₂ from glucose. Used in brewing, baking, and bioethanol production.

🥛

Lactic Acid Bacteria

Lactobacillus sp. converts glucose to lactic acid. Used in yoghurt, cheese, silage, and probiotic production.

🧪

Bacteria (Others)

Clostridium sp. produces butanol & acetone; Acetobacter converts ethanol to acetic acid (vinegar).

🍄

Moulds (Fungi)

Aspergillus sp. ferments sugars to citric acid & gluconic acid. Also produce industrial enzymes like amylase.

Enzymatic Activity in Fermentation

Enzymes act as biological catalysts that drive each step of fermentation. Click each accordion panel to explore key enzymes.

🧬 Zymase Complex (Glycolysis + Fermentation) ▼

Zymase is a collective term for the enzyme complex in yeast responsible for alcoholic fermentation. It comprises multiple enzymes working sequentially:

  • Hexokinase — phosphorylates glucose to glucose-6-phosphate
  • Phosphofructokinase — key regulatory enzyme of glycolysis
  • Pyruvate kinase — produces pyruvate from PEP
  • Pyruvate decarboxylase — converts pyruvate → acetaldehyde + CO₂
  • Alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) — reduces acetaldehyde → ethanol; regenerates NAD⁺
🌾 Amylase (Starch → Sugar) ▼

Amylase hydrolyses starch into fermentable sugars before fermentation begins (saccharification step).

  • α-Amylase — cleaves internal α-1,4 glycosidic bonds → maltodextrins (liquefaction)
  • β-Amylase — cleaves from non-reducing ends → maltose units
  • Glucoamylase — cleaves glucose units completely for direct fermentation

Produced by Aspergillus oryzae, Bacillus subtilis, and added to starch-based fermentation media.

🍬 Invertase (Sucrose → Glucose + Fructose) ▼

Invertase (β-fructofuranosidase) is secreted by S. cerevisiae and hydrolyses sucrose into glucose and fructose — both of which can be directly fermented:

  • Sucrose → Glucose + Fructose (both enter glycolysis)
  • Critical when molasses (sucrose-rich) is used as substrate
  • Allows yeast to use cane sugar and beet sugar substrates efficiently
🥛 Lactase (Lactose → Glucose + Galactose) ▼

Lactase (β-galactosidase) breaks down milk sugar (lactose) into fermentable monosaccharides:

  • Used in dairy fermentation (cheese, yoghurt, kefir production)
  • Also relevant in whey fermentation for bioethanol
  • Produced by Lactobacillus and Kluyveromyces marxianus
Types of Fermentation

🌬️ Aerobic Fermentation

  • Occurs in the presence of oxygen
  • Complete oxidation of substrate to CO₂ & H₂O
  • High ATP yield (~36–38 ATP per glucose)
  • Products: CO₂, H₂O, biomass, organic acids
  • Examples: Citric acid, vinegar, single-cell protein
  • Used for: antibiotic, enzyme, and vitamin production

🚫🌬️ Anaerobic Fermentation

  • Occurs without oxygen
  • Partial oxidation — only 2 ATP per glucose
  • NAD⁺ regenerated by product formation
  • Products: ethanol + CO₂, or lactic acid
  • Examples: Ethanol, lactic acid, biogas
  • Used for: alcohol, dairy fermentation, silage

📋 Sub-types of Anaerobic Fermentation

  • Alcoholic fermentation: Glucose → Ethanol + CO₂ (yeast)
  • Lactic acid fermentation: Glucose → Lactic acid (bacteria) — homofermentative vs. heterofermentative
  • Butyric acid fermentation: Glucose → Butyric acid + H₂ + CO₂ (Clostridium)
  • Propionic acid fermentation: Lactic acid → Propionic acid + CO₂ (Propionibacterium)

Conditions Required for Fermentation

Efficient fermentation requires careful optimisation of substrate, microbial strain, and physicochemical parameters. Any deviation from optimal conditions reduces yield significantly.

🌾
Substrate
Glucose / Sucrose / Starch
Carbon and energy source for the microorganism. Must be soluble and at optimum concentration (10–20% for ethanol).
🦠
Microorganism
S. cerevisiae (Yeast)
Must be pure culture, high-yielding strain, alcohol-tolerant, and capable of growing rapidly on available substrate.
🌡️
Temperature
25 – 35°C
Optimal for yeast: 28–32°C. High temperature denatures enzymes; low temperature slows metabolism and growth.
⚖️
pH
4.5 – 5.5
Slightly acidic pH optimal for yeast. Low pH prevents bacterial contamination. pH <4 or >6 inhibits fermentation.
🌀
Oxygen
Anaerobic / Microaerophilic
Minimal O₂ for alcoholic fermentation. Small amounts needed for yeast growth initially; then sealed for anaerobic production.
🧂
Nutrients
N, P, Vitamins, Minerals
Nitrogen source (ammonium sulfate), phosphate, potassium, magnesium, and vitamins like biotin and thiamine are essential.
Detailed Conditions — Accordion
🌾 Substrate — Types and Concentration ▼

The substrate must be rich in fermentable sugars and cost-effective. Key substrates include:

  • Molasses — by-product of sugar refining; contains 50–55% sucrose; cheap & widely used
  • Sugarcane juice — direct source of sucrose; used in Brazil and India
  • Corn/wheat starch — requires saccharification (amylase treatment) before fermentation
  • Cellulosic biomass — requires pre-treatment + cellulase; used for second-generation ethanol

Optimal sugar concentration: 10–20% w/v. Above 20%, osmotic stress inhibits yeast; below 5%, low product yield.

🌡️ Temperature — Critical Parameter ▼

Temperature directly affects enzyme activity and cell growth:

  • Optimal range for S. cerevisiae: 28–32°C
  • Below 20°C: Greatly reduced metabolic rate — slow fermentation
  • Above 40°C: Enzymes denatured; cell death; fermentation stops
  • Industrial fermenters use cooling jackets to control exothermic heat released
  • Thermotolerant strains (optimum ~38–40°C) reduce cooling costs in tropical countries
⚖️ pH — Controlling Contamination and Activity ▼

pH impacts enzyme activity and microbial competition:

  • Optimal pH for yeast fermentation: 4.5–5.5
  • pH 4.5 minimises contamination by acid-sensitive bacteria
  • pH above 6 favours bacterial contaminants (lactic acid bacteria)
  • pH is maintained using phosphate buffers or by controlled addition of H₂SO₄ / NaOH
  • As fermentation proceeds, CO₂ production naturally lowers pH (carbonic acid)
🌀 Oxygen Availability — Aerobic vs Anaerobic Phases ▼

Oxygen requirement differs by fermentation phase:

  • Phase 1 (Aerobic — Growth): Limited aeration allows yeast biomass multiplication
  • Phase 2 (Anaerobic — Production): Vessel sealed; anaerobic conditions shift yeast metabolism to ethanol production (Pasteur Effect)
  • High O₂ → Crabtree effect: yeast produces ethanol even in aerobic conditions at high glucose
  • Industrial practice: Sparging with N₂ gas to create fully anaerobic conditions in sealed bioreactors
🧂 Nutrients — Nitrogen, Minerals & Vitamins ▼

Beyond carbon (substrate), yeast requires macro- and micro-nutrients:

  • Nitrogen: Ammonium sulfate [(NH₄)₂SO₄] or urea — for amino acid and protein synthesis
  • Phosphorus: Monopotassium phosphate (KH₂PO₄) — for ATP, nucleic acids
  • Magnesium: MgSO₄ — enzyme cofactor (hexokinase, enolase)
  • Vitamins: Biotin, thiamine (B₁), pantothenic acid — for enzyme function
  • Molasses supplementation: Usually provides sufficient trace elements naturally

⚠️ Inhibitors of Fermentation

  • Ethanol toxicity: >14–15% v/v ethanol inhibits yeast growth (product inhibition)
  • Osmotic stress: Very high sugar concentration dehydrates cells
  • Contamination: Competing bacteria produce acids that lower yield
  • Heavy metals: Copper, zinc at high concentrations inhibit enzymes
  • Temperature extremes: >40°C denatures critical fermentation enzymes

📕 Chapter 5

Production of Ethanol

Ethanol (C₂H₅OH) is produced industrially through the anaerobic fermentation of sugar-rich substrates by Saccharomyces cerevisiae. It is the world's most produced biofuel.

Raw Materials
1st Generation
🍯

Molasses

By-product of sugar cane/beet processing. Contains 45–55% fermentable sugars (mainly sucrose). Cheapest and most widely used substrate in India and Brazil.

1st Generation
🎋

Sugarcane Juice

Fresh expressed juice with 12–18% sucrose. Direct and efficient substrate; Brazil's primary ethanol source (~30 billion litres/year).

1st Generation
🌽

Starch Sources

Corn, wheat, cassava, potato. Requires saccharification (enzymatic hydrolysis of starch by amylase) to glucose before fermentation. Primary substrate in USA (corn).

2nd Generation
🌿

Lignocellulosic Biomass

Agricultural waste: sugarcane bagasse, rice straw, corn stover. Requires pre-treatment + cellulase. Not yet commercially dominant but growing rapidly.

Microorganism: Saccharomyces cerevisiae

🦠 Why Saccharomyces cerevisiae (Baker's/Brewer's Yeast)?

  • High ethanol yield: Converts ~90–95% of theoretical maximum glucose to ethanol
  • Ethanol tolerance: Survives up to 14–15% v/v ethanol (flocculent strains up to 20%)
  • GRAS status: Generally Recognised As Safe — used in food and drink
  • Fast growth: Doubling time ~90 minutes under optimal conditions
  • Facultative anaerobe: Can grow both aerobically (biomass) and anaerobically (ethanol)
  • Well-characterised: Fully sequenced genome; amenable to genetic improvement
Biochemical Basis of Alcoholic Fermentation

Alcoholic fermentation proceeds in two main stages: glycolysis (in the cytoplasm) followed by pyruvate reduction to ethanol.

Step 1 — Glycolysis (Embden–Meyerhof–Parnas Pathway)
C₆H₁₂O₆ + 2 ADP + 2 Pᵢ + 2 NAD⁺ → 2 CH₃COCOOH (Pyruvate) + 2 ATP + 2 NADH + 2 H₂O
Step 2 — Decarboxylation (Pyruvate → Acetaldehyde)
CH₃COCOOH —[Pyruvate decarboxylase]→ CH₃CHO (Acetaldehyde) + CO₂
Step 3 — Reduction (Acetaldehyde → Ethanol)
CH₃CHO + NADH + H⁺ —[Alcohol dehydrogenase]→ C₂H₅OH (Ethanol) + NAD⁺
NAD⁺ regenerated here allows glycolysis to continue continuously
Overall Net Reaction
C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2 C₂H₅OH + 2 CO₂ + 2 ATP
Theoretical yield: 51.1 g ethanol per 100 g glucose (Gay-Lussac's equation)

Industrial Applications of Ethanol
⛽

Biofuel

Blended with petrol as E5, E10, E20; reduces GHG emissions. India targets E20 blend by 2025 under National Biofuel Policy.

🧴

Sanitiser & Antiseptic

70% ethanol is a potent antiseptic. Widely used in hand sanitisers, pharmaceutical cleaning, and hospital disinfection.

🧪

Industrial Solvent

Solvent for resins, dyes, perfumes, pharmaceuticals, and cosmetics. Used in extraction, printing, and textile industries.

🍸

Beverages

Base of beer (~4–5%), wine (~12%), whisky (~40%), and other alcoholic drinks produced by yeast fermentation.

⚗️

Chemical Feedstock

Precursor for acetic acid, ethyl acetate, diethyl ether, acetaldehyde, and polyethylene (bio-based plastic).

💉

Pharmaceutical

Solvent and preservative in medicines, cough syrups, tinctures, and extraction of plant-based active compounds.

🏭 Industrial Ethanol Production — Process Flow

👆 Click each step to see detailed information

1

Raw Material Preparation

Collection and processing of substrate

🌾
Raw Material Preparation:
• Molasses: Diluted with water to 10–18° Brix (sugar content); adjusted to pH 4.5–5.0
• Sugarcane: Juice expressed by crushing; clarified by settling or centrifugation
• Starch sources: Milled to flour → cooked at 120–150°C → treated with α-amylase (liquefaction) then glucoamylase (saccharification) to release glucose
• Mineral nutrients (N, P, Mg) and vitamins added to the medium
▼
2

Medium Sterilisation

Elimination of contaminating microorganisms

🔥
Medium Sterilisation:
• Heating medium to 80–100°C for 10–30 minutes kills most bacteria and wild yeast
• For molasses: often acidification to pH 3.5–4.0 is sufficient to reduce contamination risk
• Industrial fermenters use continuous sterilisation (flash heat exchange) — faster and energy-efficient
• Cooling to inoculation temperature (28–32°C) before yeast addition is essential
▼
3

Inoculation

Addition of yeast starter culture

🦠
Inoculation:
• Saccharomyces cerevisiae starter culture prepared in a seed fermenter (smaller vessel)
• Yeast pre-grown aerobically to build up high cell density (10⁷–10⁸ cells/mL)
• Inoculation rate: 2–5% v/v of the main fermenter volume
• Active Dry Yeast (ADY) or Compressed Yeast may also be used for industrial batches
• Inoculum viability must be ≥90% (checked by methylene blue or trypan blue exclusion)
▼
4

Fermentation

Anaerobic sugar-to-ethanol conversion

🏺
Fermentation Phase:
• Bioreactor sealed; anaerobic conditions maintained (N₂ sparging or CO₂ blanket)
• Temperature: 28–32°C (controlled by cooling jacket — reaction is exothermic)
• pH: maintained at 4.5–5.5
• Duration: 24–72 hours depending on substrate and strain
• Yeast converts glucose → ethanol + CO₂ via Embden–Meyerhof–Parnas pathway
• CO₂ produced (≈ 44 g per 100 g glucose) must be vented safely
• Final ethanol concentration in fermented wash: typically 8–12% v/v
▼
5

Ethanol Formation Monitoring

Process analytics and end-point detection

📊
Monitoring Parameters:
• Brix (°Bx): Measures residual sugar concentration — fermentation complete when Brix drops to <2°
• Ethanol content: Measured by ebulliometer, GC, or specific gravity
• pH: Monitored continuously; controlled by buffer additions
• Cell count: Yeast viability and density by microscopy or coulter counter
• CO₂ evolution: Rate of gas production indicates fermentation activity
• Online sensors (pH electrode, dissolved O₂ probe) used in modern plants
▼
6

Distillation

Separation of ethanol from fermented wash

🏗️
Distillation:
• Fermented wash (8–12% ethanol) pumped to distillation columns
• Ethanol boils at 78.4°C; water boils at 100°C — difference allows separation
• Analyser column: Strips ethanol from the wash; produces vapour of ~40–50% ethanol
• Rectifier column: Concentrates ethanol to 95–96% (azeotropic limit)
• Bottom product (slop/stillage) contains water, yeast, and organic residues — used as animal feed or fertiliser
• Energy integration: heat recovery systems reduce steam consumption
▼
7

Dehydration & Purification

Producing anhydrous (fuel-grade) ethanol

💎
Dehydration to Anhydrous Ethanol:
• 95–96% ethanol forms a water-ethanol azeotrope — cannot be further concentrated by simple distillation
• Methods to break azeotrope:
  – Molecular sieves (zeolite 3Å): Adsorb water selectively — most common modern method
  – Azeotropic distillation: Add benzene or cyclohexane as entrainer
  – Vacuum distillation or pervaporation membranes
• Final product: Anhydrous ethanol ≥99.5% suitable for fuel blending (E5–E20)
• Fuel ethanol is denatured with 2–5% petrol to prevent consumption

📌 Key Process Parameters Summary

  • Substrate concentration: 10–18° Brix
  • Fermentation temperature: 28–32°C
  • pH: 4.5–5.5
  • Fermentation time: 24–72 hours
  • Ethanol in wash: 8–12% v/v
  • Final ethanol purity: 95–99.5%

📖 Glossary

Key Terms & Glossary

Important scientific terms with simple, student-friendly definitions for quick revision and exam preparation.

Fermentation
The microbial metabolic process of partial anaerobic breakdown of organic compounds (sugars) to produce energy, alcohol, organic acids, or gases without the net consumption of oxygen.
Substrate
The starting material or carbon source (e.g., glucose, sucrose, starch, molasses) that microorganisms use as food and energy source during fermentation.
Inoculum
A small quantity of active starter culture of the desired microorganism added to a fermentation medium to initiate the fermentation process.
Bioreactor (Fermenter)
A controlled vessel or tank designed to provide optimal physical and chemical conditions (temperature, pH, aeration) for large-scale microbial growth and product synthesis.
Saccharomyces cerevisiae
The most widely used yeast in industrial fermentation. Also called baker's yeast or brewer's yeast; converts sugars to ethanol + CO₂ under anaerobic conditions. GRAS status.
Glycolysis (EMP Pathway)
The first stage of sugar metabolism occurring in the cytoplasm, converting one glucose (6C) into two pyruvate molecules (3C) with a net gain of 2 ATP and 2 NADH.
Zymase
The enzyme complex in yeast responsible for alcoholic fermentation. Includes key enzymes like pyruvate decarboxylase and alcohol dehydrogenase. Discovered by Eduard Buchner (1897).
Alcohol Dehydrogenase (ADH)
The enzyme that catalyses the final step of alcoholic fermentation: reducing acetaldehyde to ethanol using NADH, thereby regenerating NAD⁺ for continued glycolysis.
Molasses
A thick dark syrup by-product of sugar refining containing 45–55% fermentable sugars. The cheapest and most commonly used substrate for industrial ethanol fermentation.
Saccharification
The enzymatic hydrolysis of starch or cellulose into fermentable sugars (glucose, maltose) using amylase or cellulase enzymes as a preparatory step before fermentation.
Distillation
A separation technique using differences in boiling points to separate ethanol (bp 78.4°C) from water (bp 100°C) in the fermented wash to concentrate the product.
Azeotrope
A constant-boiling mixture of ethanol and water (95.6% ethanol: 4.4% water) that cannot be separated further by simple distillation. Dehydration requires molecular sieves or azeotropic distillation.
Pasteur Effect
The inhibition of fermentation by oxygen, observed by Louis Pasteur. In aerobic conditions, yeast uses aerobic respiration; in anaerobic conditions, it switches to fermentation for energy.
Bioethanol
Ethanol produced by biological fermentation of biomass (sugarcane, corn, cellulose) used as a renewable fuel. Blended with petrol (E5–E20) to reduce fossil fuel dependence and GHG emissions.
NAD⁺ / NADH
Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide — a crucial electron carrier coenzyme. NAD⁺ (oxidised) accepts electrons in glycolysis to form NADH. In fermentation, NADH is re-oxidised back to NAD⁺, allowing glycolysis to continue.
Anhydrous Ethanol
Ethanol with ≥99.5% purity (virtually water-free), produced by molecular sieve dehydration. Required for fuel-grade ethanol to prevent engine corrosion and phase separation when blended with petrol.
