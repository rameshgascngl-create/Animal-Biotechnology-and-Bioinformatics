Human Genome Project, Ethics and IPR

Human Genome Project · Bioethics · Intellectual Property Rights

The Human Genome Project gave us the "parts list" of a human being. With that power come questions of privacy, fairness and ownership. This module covers the mapping and sequencing of the human genome, its applications, the ethical issues of biotechnology and the basics of intellectual property rights.

📗 Chapter 1

Human Genome Project (HGP): Overview

The HGP read the complete DNA sequence of a human being, one of the largest collaborative projects in biology.

Human Genome Project
An international research programme (1990–2003) to determine the sequence of the roughly 3 billion base pairs of the human genome, identify its genes, and develop tools and ethical guidelines for using the information.
1990
Officially launched
2003
Completed (99% of euchromatic DNA)
~3.1 Gb
Haploid genome size
~20,000
Protein-coding genes
Timeline
1953 — Watson and Crick propose the structure of DNA
1977 — Sanger and Maxam–Gilbert sequencing methods
1990 — HGP formally begins (US DOE and NIH; James Watson first head of the NIH genome office; Francis Collins later led it)
1998 — Celera Genomics (Craig Venter) starts a private shotgun effort
June 2000 — Joint announcement of a working draft
Feb 2001 — Draft published in Nature (public consortium) and Science (Celera)
April 2003 — Sequence declared essentially complete, 50 years after the DNA double helix
2022 — Telomere-to-Telomere (T2T) consortium publishes the first truly gapless human genome sequence
Goals of the project
  • Identify all human genes (~20,000–25,000 estimated then).
  • Determine the sequence of the 3 billion base pairs.
  • Store the data in public databases.
  • Improve tools for data analysis.
  • Transfer technologies to industry.
  • Address the ethical, legal and social implications (ELSI); 3–5% of the budget was devoted to ELSI research.

Who took part?

The International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium: USA, UK (Wellcome Trust, Sanger Centre), France, Germany, Japan and China, with data released freely on the internet. India contributed through scientists and later its own population genome studies.

ESTs and STS

Expressed Sequence Tags (ESTs) are short sequences from cDNA that mark expressed genes. Sequence-tagged sites (STSs) are short, unique, PCR-amplifiable sequences used as landmarks in the physical map.

📙 Chapter 3

Salient Findings and Applications of the HGP

The sequence revealed how few genes we have and how much of the genome is not coding.

Key findings
FindingDetail
Gene number≈ 20,000 protein-coding genes (far fewer than the 100,000 once predicted)
Coding DNAOnly about 1–2% of the genome codes for proteins
RepeatsAbout half is repetitive sequence; Alu (a SINE) is the most abundant repeat (~10%)
VariationAny two people are about 99.9% identical; SNPs occur about once per 1,000 bases
ChromosomesChromosome 1 is the largest; chromosome 21 is the smallest autosome; Y has few genes
Gene densityUneven: gene-rich regions and large gene deserts
Applications
🧬

Medicine

  • Identification of disease genes (cystic fibrosis, Huntington's).
  • Predictive and prenatal genetic tests.
  • Pharmacogenomics and personalised medicine.
  • Gene therapy targets.
🔎

Forensics

  • DNA profiling and identification.
  • Paternity testing.
🌾

Agriculture & environment

  • Genome projects of crops and livestock.
  • Microbial genomics for bioremediation.
💭

Evolution

  • Comparative genomics with chimpanzee and other species.
  • Tracing human migrations.
💻

Bioinformatics

  • Created the demand for databases and tools (GenBank, BLAST).
🌎

Population genomics

  • National projects such as GenomeIndia (DBT) map the genetic diversity of Indian populations.

After the HGP

Cheap next-generation sequencing now sequences a genome in hours for a few hundred dollars, enabling projects such as the 1000 Genomes Project, ENCODE and cancer genome atlases.

📒 Chapter 4

Ethics in Biotechnology: Socio-ethical Problems

Technology raises questions of what we can do versus what we should do.

Bioethics
The study of moral issues arising from biological research and its applications. Four core principles: autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence and justice.
🔒
Genetic privacy and discrimination▼
  • Genetic information is highly personal and can reveal family risks.
  • Risk of discrimination by insurers or employers; in the USA the GINA (2008) law prohibits it in health insurance and employment.
  • Need for confidentiality and secure data storage.
✍
Informed consent▼
  • Participants must know the risks, benefits and uses of data before agreeing.
  • Special care for children, the mentally ill and indigenous communities.
👶
Germline editing and "designer babies"▼
  • Changes are inherited by future generations without their consent.
  • In 2018, He Jiankui announced the birth of gene-edited twins; the scientific community condemned this and he was imprisoned in China.
  • Germline editing for reproduction is prohibited in many countries.
🌽
GM food and environment▼
  • Concerns: allergenicity, gene flow to wild relatives, loss of biodiversity, resistance in pests.
  • Right to know: labelling of GM foods.
🐐
Animal welfare▼
  • The 3Rs: Replace, Reduce, Refine.
  • Institutional Animal Ethics Committees (CPCSEA in India) approve experiments.
⚖
Justice and access▼
  • Gene therapies and biologics can cost millions; unequal access between rich and poor.
  • Benefit-sharing with communities that supply genetic resources.
💀
Dual use and bioweapons▼
  • Research with legitimate benefits may be misused; oversight needed.

Intellectual Property Rights (IPR)

IPR gives inventors exclusive rights over their creations, encouraging investment in biotechnology while raising debates about patenting life.

Intellectual property rights
Legal rights granted to a creator or inventor to exclude others from using an intellectual creation for a fixed period, so that they can recover investment and profit from their work.
Forms of IPR
FormProtectsDuration (typical)Biotechnology example
PatentInventions (products, processes)20 years from filing (TRIPS)Recombinant insulin process, PCR, transgenic mouse method
CopyrightOriginal literary/artistic works, softwareLife + 60 years (India)Bioinformatics software, textbooks
TrademarkBrand names, logos10 years, renewableBrand name of a drug (Herceptin)
Trade secretConfidential business informationAs long as secretMedia formulations of a company
Geographical Indication (GI)Products from a region with special quality10 years, renewableDarjeeling tea, Basmati rice
Plant breeders' rightsNew plant varieties15–18 yearsNew hybrid variety (PPV&FR Act, 2001)
Patent criteria
💡

Novelty

Not previously known or published anywhere.

🔄

Inventive step

Not obvious to a person skilled in the art.

🏭

Industrial applicability

Capable of being made or used in industry.

Patenting micro-organisms: Budapest Treaty

The Budapest Treaty (1977) recognises the deposit of a micro-organism in an International Depositary Authority as part of the patent application. In India, a deposit centre is IMTECH, Chandigarh (MTCC).

Important international and Indian instruments
TRIPS (1995) — WTO agreement setting minimum IPR standards; 20-year patent term
Convention on Biological Diversity (1992) — Sovereign rights over biological resources; access and benefit sharing (Nagoya Protocol, 2010)
Indian Patents Act, 1970 — Amended in 2005 to allow product patents; Section 3(j) excludes plants, animals, seeds, varieties and essentially biological processes; Section 3(d) restricts patents on new forms of known substances without enhanced efficacy
Biological Diversity Act, 2002 — National Biodiversity Authority (Chennai) regulates access to biological resources
PPV&FR Act, 2001 — Protects plant varieties and farmers' rights
Landmark cases
CaseIssueOutcome

Harvard Oncomouse (1988)First patented transgenic animal (USA)Granted in the USA; contested in Canada and Europe
Neem patent (EPO)Patent on antifungal use of neem oil (W. R. Grace and USDA)Revoked in 2000 after challenge by India and others

AMP v. Myriad (2013)Patent on isolated human BRCA genesUS Supreme Court: natural DNA not patentable; cDNA is

Biopiracy

Biopiracy is the unauthorised commercial use of biological resources or traditional knowledge without sharing benefits. India's Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL) documents traditional knowledge to prevent wrongful patents.

Q.1What is the Human Genome Project? State its goals.▼

An international project (1990–2003) to sequence the ~3 billion base pairs of the human genome, identify its genes, store data in databases, develop analysis tools, and study the ethical, legal and social implications.

Q.2Differentiate genetic and physical maps.▼

A genetic map is based on recombination frequency (cM) between markers; a physical map shows actual distance in base pairs using restriction sites, STS and clone contigs.

Q.3Compare the public and Celera approaches.▼

The public consortium used hierarchical clone-by-clone BAC sequencing guided by maps; Celera used whole-genome shotgun sequencing followed by computer assembly.

Q.4List four applications of HGP.▼

Identification of disease genes, predictive testing and pharmacogenomics, forensic DNA profiling, comparative genomics/evolution, development of bioinformatics.

Q.5What are the ethical issues related to genetic information?▼

Privacy, discrimination by insurers/employers, informed consent, genetic testing of minors, germline editing, access and equity.

Q.6What is a patent? What are the criteria for patentability?▼

A patent is an exclusive right for 20 years to an inventor in exchange for disclosure. The invention must be novel, involve an inventive step and be industrially applicable.

Q.7What is biopiracy? Give an example.▼

Unauthorised exploitation of biological resources or traditional knowledge without benefit sharing. Example: the patents on neem and turmeric, later revoked.

📖 Glossary

Key Terms & Glossary

Important terms in simple language.

Genome
The complete set of DNA of an organism.
Contig
A set of overlapping DNA fragments forming a continuous sequence.
BAC
Bacterial artificial chromosome, a vector carrying 100–200 kb inserts.
STS
Sequence-tagged site: short unique DNA sequence used as a map landmark.
SNP
Single nucleotide polymorphism: a single-base difference between individuals.
ELSI
Ethical, Legal and Social Implications of genomics research.
Informed consent
Voluntary agreement after understanding risks and benefits.
Patent
Exclusive right to an invention for a fixed period (20 years).
TRIPS
WTO agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights.
Biopiracy
Unauthorised commercial use of biological resources or traditional knowledge.
GEAC
Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee, India's regulator of GMO release.
Geographical Indication
Tag for products with qualities linked to their region of origin.
