Branches and Milestones of Biotechnology

A single picture of what biotechnology covers, and the key dates every student must remember.

Biotechnology
The use of living organisms, cells or their components to make products or processes for human use (Karl Ereky coined the term in 1919; the Convention on Biological Diversity gives a formal modern definition).
Fig. 1. Colour-coded branches of biotechnology (a common convention; some authors add further colours).
Fig. 2. Milestones. Click-free summary: 1973 Cohen and Boyer made the first recombinant DNA; 1975 Köhler and Milstein made hybridomas; 1977 Sanger sequencing.

PG focus

  • Distinguish traditional biotechnology (fermentation, breeding) from modern biotechnology (rDNA, cell fusion, gene editing).
  • Know why the 1975 Asilomar meeting is a landmark for biosafety.

Enzymes: Cutting, Joining and Copying DNA

Restriction enzymes are molecular scissors; ligase is the glue. Know how ends are produced.

Fig. 3. EcoRI leaves 5′ AATT overhangs; SmaI leaves blunt ends. Red lines mark the cut positions.
TypeCofactorsRecognition / cutUse
Type IATP, Mg²⁺, SAMCut far from the recognition site (random)Not used in cloning
Type IIMg²⁺ onlyCut at or near a palindromic site (4–8 bp)Standard in cloning (EcoRI, BamHI, HindIII)
Type IIIATP, Mg²⁺Cut 20–30 bp from the siteRare
Fig. 4. The enzyme toolkit and the job each enzyme does.

Exam tip

Isoschizomers recognise the same sequence (SmaI and XmaI); neoschizomers cut the same sequence at a different position (SmaI blunt, XmaI sticky). Compatible cohesive ends (BamHI and BglII) can be ligated but the junction is no longer cut by either enzyme.

Plasmid Vectors: pBR322 and the Ti Plasmid

Plasmids are small, circular, self-replicating DNA molecules. Learn the map of pBR322 and the Ti plasmid structure.

Features of a good plasmid vector: an origin of replication, one or more selectable markers, unique restriction sites (multiple cloning site), small size, and high copy number.

Fig. 5. pBR322 (4,361 bp). Sites inside tetR (BamHI, SalI, HindIII) allow insertional inactivation of tetracycline resistance; PstI, PvuI and ScaI lie in ampR.
Insert atGene inactivatedRecombinant phenotype
BamHI / SalI / HindIIItetRAmpR TetS
PstI / PvuI / ScaIampRAmpS TetR
EcoRInone (outside both genes)AmpR TetR; needs another screen
Fig. 6. Ti plasmid of Agrobacterium tumefaciens: the T-DNA lies between the right (RB) and left (LB) borders; vir genes act in trans. Schematic, not to scale.

Ti plasmid in brief

  • Wounded plant cells release phenolics (acetosyringone) that activate VirA/VirG.
  • VirD1/D2 nick the borders; the T-strand (with VirE2) passes through the VirB type IV secretion system into the plant nucleus.
  • In disarmed vectors the oncogenes are removed and the gene of interest is placed between the borders.
  • Relevance to animal biotechnology: a classic example of natural genetic engineering and of a binary vector design.

Cosmid, YAC, BAC and Animal Virus Vectors

Genomes are big, so large-insert vectors are needed; animal cells need their own vectors.

Fig. 10. Cloning capacity (kb, log scale). Pick the vector by the size of DNA you must carry.
Fig. 11. Cosmid cloning combines plasmid replication with phage packaging.
Fig. 12. A YAC after ligation to genomic DNA. It behaves as a mini chromosome in yeast.
Fig. 13. SV40, a small DNA tumour virus used to build mammalian expression vectors.
Fig. 14. Transposons (Sleeping Beauty, piggyBac) as cut-and-paste vectors for animal cells.

PG focus

  • YACs suffer from chimerism and instability; BACs (F-factor based, 100–300 kb) became the workhorse of the Human Genome Project.
  • Lentiviral and AAV vectors have largely replaced SV40 in gene therapy.

📑 Atlas 1.6

DNA Libraries: Genomic versus cDNA

A library is a collection of cloned fragments representing a genome or the mRNA of a tissue.

Fig. 15. Two library strategies.
FeatureGenomic librarycDNA library
SourceGenomic DNA (any cell)mRNA of a tissue or stage
ContainsIntrons, promoters, regulatory DNAExons of expressed genes only
SizeDepends on genomeDepends on expressed genes
UseGene structure and regulation, genome mappingExpression of eukaryotic genes in bacteria

Worked example

Number of clones needed: N = ln(1 − P) / ln(1 − f), where P is the probability (0.99) and f is the insert size ÷ genome size. For the human genome (3.1 × 10⁹ bp) with 20 kb inserts: f = 6.5 × 10⁻⁶, so N ≈ 7 × 10⁵ clones.

Summary & Exam Revision

Key points and model answers likely to appear in university examinations.

Key exam points
1
Colour code: red medical, green agricultural, white industrial, blue marine.
2
Type II restriction enzymes cut palindromes; EcoRI gives 5′ sticky ends, SmaI gives blunt ends.
3
Ligase joins; phosphatase stops self-ligation; reverse transcriptase makes cDNA.
4
pBR322: 4,361 bp; ampR, tetR, ori, rop; BamHI/SalI in tet, PstI in amp.
5
Ti plasmid: T-DNA between LB and RB, vir genes act in trans; disarmed vectors carry the gene of interest.
6
Lambda 48.5 kb: lytic or lysogenic; replacement vectors carry ~20 kb. M13: ssDNA, non-lytic.
7
Capacity: plasmid ≤10 kb; lambda ≈ 20–23 kb; cosmid 35–45 kb; BAC/PAC 100–300 kb; YAC 200–2,000 kb.
8
SV40 (5.2 kb) and transposons (Sleeping Beauty, piggyBac) are animal-cell vectors.
9
Library size: N = ln(1−P) / ln(1−f).
Short-answer questions

Click a question to reveal the model answer.

Q.1Draw and label the cutting of DNA by EcoRI.▼

EcoRI recognises 5′-GAATTC-3′ (palindromic), cuts between G and A on both strands, and leaves 5′ AATT overhangs (sticky ends) that anneal to any other EcoRI-cut DNA.

Q.2Name four features of an ideal cloning vector.▼

Origin of replication, selectable marker, unique restriction sites (MCS), small size, high copy number, ability to be introduced into the host.

Q.3Explain insertional inactivation in pBR322.▼

Foreign DNA is inserted in a marker gene (for example BamHI in tet). The recombinant loses that resistance but keeps the other (Amp-R Tet-S), identified by replica plating.

Q.4Differentiate lytic and lysogenic cycles.▼

Lytic: phage replicates, lyses the cell, releases progeny. Lysogenic: DNA integrates as a prophage, repressed by cI, and replicates with the host until induction.

Q.5What is a cosmid and why is it useful?▼

A plasmid containing lambda cos sites that can be packaged in vitro; carries 35–45 kb inserts and is useful for genomic libraries.

Q.6Compare genomic and cDNA libraries.▼

Genomic libraries contain all DNA (introns and regulatory sequences); cDNA libraries contain only exons of genes expressed in the source tissue, made via reverse transcriptase.

Q.7Why are YACs used for large genomes?▼

They behave as yeast chromosomes (CEN, ARS, TEL) and carry up to 2,000 kb inserts, reducing the number of clones needed.

📖 Glossary

Key Terms & Glossary

Important terms in simple language.

Palindrome
DNA sequence reading the same 5′→3′ on both strands.
Sticky end
Single-stranded overhang left by a staggered cut.
Blunt end
Flush end with no overhang.
MCS
Multiple cloning site: cluster of unique restriction sites.
Selectable marker
Gene (for example antibiotic resistance) used to select cells carrying the vector.
Prophage
Phage genome integrated into the host chromosome.
cos site
Cohesive end site of lambda used for packaging and circularisation.
T-DNA
Segment of the Ti plasmid transferred to plant cells.
BAC
Bacterial artificial chromosome based on the F plasmid.
YAC
Yeast artificial chromosome with CEN, ARS and TEL.
cDNA
DNA copied from mRNA by reverse transcriptase.
Transposon
Mobile DNA element that moves by cut-and-paste or copy-and-paste.
