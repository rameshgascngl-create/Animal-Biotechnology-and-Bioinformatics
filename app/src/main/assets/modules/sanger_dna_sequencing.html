DNA Sequencing: The Sanger Method

Chain Termination · Dideoxynucleotides · Fragment Separation · Sequence Reading

Knowing the precise order of nucleotides in a DNA molecule was the dream that unlocked the entire genome era. Frederick Sanger's elegant chain-termination method — awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1980 — made that dream a reality and became the gold standard of DNA sequencing for over three decades.

Section 1

🧬 Introduction to DNA Sequencing

What Is DNA Sequencing?

DNA sequencing is the process of determining the precise order of the four nucleotide bases — Adenine (A), Thymine (T), Guanine (G), and Cytosine (C) — along a strand of DNA. The resulting sequence is the "primary structure" of that DNA molecule.

Importance in Molecular Biology, Biotechnology, and Genetic Analysis

  • Gene identification: Reveals the coding sequences, regulatory regions, and boundaries of genes.
  • Mutation detection: Identifies single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and disease-causing mutations.
  • Cloning verification: Confirms that the correct gene was cloned into the vector and no errors were introduced by PCR.
  • Genome projects: The entire Human Genome Project (3.2 Gb) was sequenced using Sanger sequencing.
  • Evolutionary studies: Comparing sequences across species reveals phylogenetic relationships.
  • Forensics and diagnostics: Pathogen identification, cancer mutation profiling.

Introduction to the Sanger Method

The Sanger method (also called the chain-termination method or dideoxy method) was developed by Frederick Sanger and colleagues at the MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology, Cambridge, in 1977. It exploits the ability of DNA polymerase to synthesise a complementary copy of a template strand — but uses specially modified nucleotides that terminate synthesis at known positions, producing a series of DNA fragments whose sizes reveal the sequence.

Nobel Prize: Frederick Sanger was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1980 for developing this method — his second Nobel Prize (he had won his first in 1958 for protein sequencing). Sanger is one of only four people to have received two Nobel Prizes.
Section 2

⚗️ Principle of the Sanger Method

Chain Termination Principle

The Sanger method is based on the chain termination (or dideoxy termination) principle: when a dideoxynucleotide triphosphate (ddNTP) is incorporated into a growing DNA chain by DNA polymerase, synthesis stops immediately — the chain is "terminated." This produces a population of DNA fragments of all possible lengths, each ending at a specific nucleotide type.

Role of DNA Polymerase

DNA polymerase (modified DNA Pol I — Klenow fragment, or thermostable Taq/T7 polymerase) reads the template strand in the 3'→5' direction and synthesises a new complementary strand in the 5'→3' direction by adding dNTPs to the 3'-OH of the growing chain. It requires a primer to initiate synthesis.

Role of Normal Deoxynucleotides (dNTPs)

The four standard deoxynucleotide triphosphates — dATP, dTTP, dGTP, dCTP — are incorporated normally into the growing chain. Each dNTP provides:

  • The nucleobase for specific base pairing with the template.
  • The deoxyribose sugar with a free 3'-OH group that allows the next nucleotide to be added.
  • Three phosphate groups — the outer two are cleaved to release energy for bond formation.

Role of Dideoxynucleotides (ddNTPs)

The four dideoxynucleotide triphosphates — ddATP, ddTTP, ddGTP, ddCTP — are chemically modified analogues of normal dNTPs. They are identical to dNTPs except that the 3'-OH group on the deoxyribose sugar is replaced by a hydrogen atom (3'-H).

Why Chain Elongation Stops When ddNTP Is Added

DNA synthesis requires a free 3'-OH group on the last nucleotide to form a new 3'–5' phosphodiester bond with the incoming nucleotide. When a ddNTP is incorporated:

  • It is paired with the complementary template base (e.g., ddATP pairs with T on template).
  • The ddNTP is added to the 3' end of the growing chain via its 5'-phosphate.
  • However, the ddNTP has no 3'-OH group (only a 3'-H).
  • Without a 3'-OH, DNA polymerase cannot form the next phosphodiester bond.
  • Result: DNA synthesis terminates permanently at that position.
Key Equation: dNTP (3'-OH) → chain continues  |  ddNTP (3'-H, no OH) → chain TERMINATES immediately.
Section 3

🧪 Components Required for Sanger Sequencing

1. Template DNA
The single-stranded or denatured double-stranded DNA whose sequence is to be determined. Must be pure and in sufficient quantity. Can be a plasmid, PCR product, or genomic fragment.
2. Primer
A short synthetic oligonucleotide (18–25 nt) complementary to the template. Provides the free 3'-OH needed for DNA polymerase to start synthesis. Defines the starting point and orientation of sequencing.
3. DNA Polymerase
Synthesises the new complementary strand. Requires high fidelity and processivity. Klenow fragment (original), T7 DNA polymerase, or Taq polymerase (cycle sequencing). Lacks 5'→3' exonuclease activity (Klenow/T7).
4. dNTPs (all four)
dATP, dTTP, dGTP, dCTP — the normal building blocks for DNA synthesis. Incorporated throughout the growing chain, providing 3'-OH for chain elongation.
5. ddNTPs (chain terminators)
ddATP, ddTTP, ddGTP, ddCTP — added at low concentration relative to dNTPs. Each terminates the chain when incorporated. In modern fluorescent Sanger, each ddNTP carries a different fluorescent dye.
6. Buffer & Conditions
Appropriate buffer (Tris-HCl, pH 7.5–8.0), MgCl₂ (cofactor for polymerase), denaturing agent (formamide for gel), reaction temperature (37°C for Klenow; 96°C denaturation for cycle sequencing).
Key Ratio: ddNTPs are added at a concentration approximately 100× lower than dNTPs. This ensures that ddNTPs are incorporated randomly and infrequently — producing fragments of all possible lengths, not just very short ones.
Section 4

🪜 Steps in Sanger Sequencing

1
Denaturation of Template DNA

The double-stranded template DNA is denatured into single strands by heating (95–96°C) or by treatment with alkali (NaOH). Single-stranded template is essential because DNA polymerase can only copy a single-stranded template.

2
Primer Annealing

A specific oligonucleotide primer (18–25 nt) is annealed to the template at a complementary sequence upstream of the region to be sequenced. The temperature is lowered (50–60°C) to allow hydrogen bonding between the primer and template.

3
DNA Synthesis by DNA Polymerase

DNA polymerase extends the primer by adding nucleotides complementary to the template in the 5'→3' direction. All four dNTPs and a small amount of all four ddNTPs are present simultaneously in a single reaction tube (modern fluorescent Sanger method). In the original radioactive method, four separate reactions were used, each containing one type of ddNTP.

4
Random Incorporation of ddNTPs (Chain Termination)

At each position in the growing chain, the polymerase may incorporate either the normal dNTP (chain continues) or the ddNTP analogue (chain terminates). Because ddNTPs lack a 3'-OH group, synthesis stops permanently at that nucleotide. The random but statistical nature of incorporation means that termination occurs at every possible position in the sequence.

5
Formation of DNA Fragments of Different Lengths

The sequencing reaction generates a ladder of DNA fragments — a population of molecules that all start at the primer and end at every possible position in the template (wherever a ddNTP was incorporated). Each fragment has a defined length and ends with a specific ddNTP:

  • Fragments ending in ddATP → terminated at every A position in the new strand.
  • Fragments ending in ddTTP → terminated at every T position.
  • Fragments ending in ddGTP → terminated at every G position.
  • Fragments ending in ddCTP → terminated at every C position.
6
Separation by Gel or Capillary Electrophoresis

The mixture of fragments is separated by size using:

  • Denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE): Original method. Formamide denatures secondary structures. Fragments migrate through the gel matrix — smaller fragments move faster and migrate farther from the wells. Typically resolves fragments from ~10 to ~1000 nt with single-nucleotide resolution.
  • Capillary electrophoresis (CE): Modern automated method. DNA fragments migrate through a thin capillary tube filled with polymer. Fragments pass a laser detector — each ddNTP carries a different fluorescent dye and emits a different colour when excited.
7
Reading of DNA Sequence

Gel method: The pattern of bands on the gel is read from bottom (smallest = shortest = closest to the primer end) to top (largest = furthest from primer). The lane containing each band reveals the terminal base at that position.

Fluorescent capillary method (modern): The laser detector records fluorescence colour in real time as fragments pass. The output is a chromatogram (electropherogram) — coloured peaks, each colour corresponding to one base. The sequence is read from left (small fragments, earliest detected) to right (larger fragments, later detected).

Section 5

📊 Interpretation of Results

Reading Fragment Sizes from a Gel

In the original radioactive Sanger method, four reactions (A, T, G, C) are run in parallel lanes on a denaturing gel. Radioactively labelled fragments appear as dark bands. The sequence is read from the bottom of the gel upwards — the lowest band (smallest fragment, i.e., the one nearest the primer) is read first.

At each band position, you record the lane (A, T, G, or C) in which the band appears. Reading upward from position 1 to the top gives the sequence of the new (complementary) strand in the 5'→3' direction.

Modern Fluorescent Chromatogram (Electropherogram)

In automated fluorescent Sanger sequencing (Applied Biosystems ABI 3730xl system):

  • A single reaction contains all four ddNTPs, each labelled with a different fluorescent dye: typically ddA = green, ddT = red, ddG = blue, ddC = purple/black.
  • Fragments separated by capillary electrophoresis pass a laser — each emits its specific colour.
  • The detector records a chromatogram: coloured peaks on a time axis.
  • Each peak represents one nucleotide position in the sequence.
  • The sequence is read from left (smallest fragments = earliest positions) to right.
  • Base-calling software (e.g., Phred) automatically reads the sequence and assigns quality scores.
Read length: A single Sanger run typically provides 600–1000 reliable base calls per reaction, with accuracy greater than 99.99% for high-quality regions.

Sequence Determination from the Template

The sequence obtained is that of the newly synthesised strand (complementary to the template), read in the 5'→3' direction. To obtain the template strand sequence, take the reverse complement.

Section 8

⚖️ Advantages and Limitations

✅ Advantages

  • High accuracy: Error rate <0.001%; considered the "gold standard" for accuracy.
  • Long read length: 600–1000 reliable bases per run.
  • Well-established: Decades of optimisation; robust and reproducible.
  • Cost-effective for small targets: Ideal for sequencing single genes, verifying clones, detecting point mutations.
  • Widely used: Standard method in clinical diagnostics, forensics, and research labs.
  • Automated: ABI 3730xl can run 384 samples simultaneously.

⚠️ Limitations

  • Limited throughput: Maximum ~1 kb per reaction; sequencing an entire genome requires millions of reactions.
  • Expensive for whole genomes: The Human Genome Project (Sanger-based) cost ~$3 billion.
  • Cannot sequence GC-rich or repetitive regions easily: Secondary structures cause compression artefacts.
  • First ~15–40 bases unreliable (primer region — poor resolution at very short lengths).
  • Requires pure, single template: Mixtures of sequences give ambiguous chromatograms.

Brief Comparison with Modern Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS)

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies (Illumina, PacBio, Oxford Nanopore) can sequence billions of base pairs simultaneously at very low cost (~$0.001/Mb vs ~$0.50/Mb for Sanger). However, Sanger remains the preferred method for validating single-gene mutations, confirming cloning results, and clinical diagnostic applications requiring high accuracy and long reads.
Section 9

🏥 Applications of Sanger Sequencing

Gene Analysis
Determining the complete nucleotide sequence of a gene, its intron-exon organisation, promoter, and regulatory elements.
Mutation Detection
Identifying single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), point mutations, insertions, and deletions associated with genetic diseases (e.g., cystic fibrosis, sickle cell disease, BRCA1/2 in cancer).
Confirmation of Cloned DNA
Verifying that a gene was correctly inserted into a vector during cloning and that no PCR errors or mutations were introduced.
Human Genome Project
Sanger sequencing was the primary technology used to sequence the entire 3.2 Gb human genome (1990–2003), leading to the identification of ~20,000–25,000 human genes.
Medical Diagnostics
Clinical sequencing of pathogen genomes (HIV drug resistance monitoring, SARS-CoV-2 variant identification), BRCA1/2 testing, pharmacogenomics.
Forensic Science
DNA fingerprinting and identification using mitochondrial DNA sequencing for forensic investigations and species identification.

🎮 Interactive Learning

Core Principle
  • ddNTPs lack a 3'-OH group (they have 3'-H instead).
  • DNA polymerase requires a 3'-OH to catalyse the next phosphodiester bond.
  • When ddNTP is incorporated → chain terminates permanently at that position.
  • dNTPs are added at ~100× higher concentration than ddNTPs — ensures all possible fragment lengths are produced.
  • The sequence is determined by which ddNTP terminated each fragment and the fragment's length.
Exam tip: The ONLY structural difference between dNTP and ddNTP is the 3' position: dNTP has 3'-OH, ddNTP has 3'-H. This single difference is the entire basis of Sanger sequencing.

Section 14

📖 Glossary

DNA Sequencing
The process of determining the precise order of nucleotide bases (A, T, G, C) along a DNA strand. The sequence reveals the genetic information encoded in the DNA molecule.
Sanger Method
A DNA sequencing technique developed by Frederick Sanger in 1977, based on the chain-termination principle using dideoxynucleotides. Also called the dideoxy method. Gold standard of DNA sequencing for over 30 years.
ddNTP (Dideoxynucleotide)
A synthetic analogue of a normal deoxyribonucleotide triphosphate (dNTP), lacking a 3'-OH group (replaced by 3'-H). When incorporated by DNA polymerase, it permanently terminates chain elongation. Used as chain terminators in Sanger sequencing.
Chain Termination
The permanent halting of DNA synthesis caused by incorporation of a ddNTP. Since ddNTPs lack a 3'-OH group, no new phosphodiester bond can be formed — the growing chain cannot be extended further.
Template DNA
The single-stranded DNA molecule used as the blueprint in DNA synthesis. In Sanger sequencing, the template strand is read 3'→5' by DNA polymerase, which synthesises a new complementary strand in the 5'→3' direction.
Primer
A short (18–25 nt) synthetic single-stranded oligonucleotide complementary to the template strand. Provides the free 3'-OH required for DNA polymerase to initiate synthesis. Defines the start and direction of sequencing in Sanger method.
Electrophoresis
A technique that uses an electric field to separate charged molecules (such as DNA fragments) by size. Smaller fragments migrate faster and farther in the gel or capillary. Enables single-nucleotide resolution of Sanger sequencing fragments.
Chromatogram (Electropherogram)
The graphical output of automated fluorescent Sanger sequencing showing coloured peaks on a time axis. Each peak represents one nucleotide — the peak colour identifies the base (A/T/G/C) and peak position reflects fragment size.
Capillary Electrophoresis
A modern, automated separation method used in Sanger sequencing where DNA fragments migrate through a thin polymer-filled capillary tube. Laser detection of fluorescence allows high-resolution, high-throughput sequencing (e.g., ABI 3730xl platform).
dNTP (Deoxynucleotide)
Deoxynucleoside triphosphate — the normal building block of DNA. Contains a deoxyribose sugar with a free 3'-OH group that allows continued chain elongation. Four types: dATP, dTTP, dGTP, dCTP.
