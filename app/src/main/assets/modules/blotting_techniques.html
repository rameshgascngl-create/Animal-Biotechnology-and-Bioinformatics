Blotting Techniques: Southern, Northern and Western

Southern, Northern and Western Blotting

A genome holds billions of bases and a cell holds thousands of proteins. Blotting lets us pick out one DNA fragment, one RNA or one protein from that crowd. This module explains the three classical blots, how they work, and when to use each.

📗 Chapter 1

Blotting Techniques: Principle & Overview

Blotting transfers separated biomolecules from a gel onto a solid membrane, where a specific probe can find one target among thousands.

Blotting is a group of techniques in which macromolecules (DNA, RNA or protein) separated by gel electrophoresis are transferred to a membrane and then detected with a labelled probe. The membrane is a durable, accessible copy of the gel.

Definition
Blotting is the transfer of electrophoretically separated nucleic acids or proteins from a gel onto a membrane (nitrocellulose, nylon or PVDF), followed by detection of a specific target with a labelled probe.
Why transfer to a membrane?
  • A gel is fragile and probes cannot diffuse into it efficiently.
  • A membrane immobilises the bands in the same pattern as the gel.
  • The same membrane can be probed repeatedly (stripped and re-probed).
  • Sensitivity is high: a single gene in a whole genome can be detected.

Where do the names come from?

A scientific pun

  • Southern blot (1975) — named after its inventor, Edwin M. Southern. It is not named after a direction.
  • Northern and Western blots were named by analogy (wordplay) for RNA and protein detection.

📘 Chapter 2

Southern Blotting (DNA)

Southern blotting detects a specific DNA sequence in a complex DNA sample such as the whole genome.

Southern blot
A method to detect a specific DNA fragment among many by transferring size-separated, denatured DNA from an agarose gel to a membrane and hybridising it with a labelled complementary probe.
Edwin M. Southern, 1975
Principle

Single-stranded DNA on the membrane forms hydrogen-bonded hybrids only with a probe of complementary sequence. After washing away unbound probe, the label shows where the target fragment lies.

Key points to remember
🧪
Probes and labels▼
  • A probe is a short single-stranded DNA/RNA complementary to the target.
  • Radioactive label: 32P (very sensitive, hazardous).
  • Non-radioactive: digoxigenin (DIG), biotin, fluorescence — safer and widely used now.
🔥
Stringency▼
  • Stringency = how strictly the probe must match to stay bound.
  • It is raised by higher temperature and lower salt concentration.
  • High stringency discards mismatched hybrids and reduces false positives.
🧰
Why alkali denaturation?▼
Double-stranded DNA is not efficiently retained by the membrane and cannot hybridise with the probe. Single strands bind and leave bases free to pair with the probe.
Applications of Southern blotting
  • Detecting a particular gene or confirming a cloned insert in a recombinant clone.
  • Checking transgene integration and copy number in transgenic animals/plants.
  • Restriction mapping and RFLP analysis.
  • DNA fingerprinting (VNTR analysis) — paternity and forensic testing.
  • Detecting gene rearrangements, deletions and amplification in genetic disease and cancer.

📙 Chapter 3

Northern Blotting (RNA)

Northern blotting measures the size and abundance of a specific RNA, so it shows whether a gene is expressed.

Northern blot
A technique for detecting a specific RNA (usually mRNA) in a sample by size-separation on a denaturing gel, transfer to a membrane and hybridisation with a labelled probe.
Alwine, Kemp and Stark, 1977
How it differs from Southern blotting
StepSouthernNorthern
Starting materialGenomic DNA, restriction-digestedTotal RNA or poly(A)+ mRNA (no digestion)
GelOrdinary agaroseFormaldehyde (or glyoxal) denaturing agarose
Alkali treatmentYes (NaOH)No — alkali hydrolyses RNA
PrecautionsRoutineStrict RNase-free technique: DEPC-treated water, gloves

Why a denaturing gel?

RNA is single-stranded but folds into secondary structures (hairpins). Denaturants keep it unfolded so that migration depends on length only and size can be read against an RNA ladder.

Western Blotting (Protein)

Western blotting identifies a specific protein in a mixture using antibodies. It is also called immunoblotting.

Western blot (immunoblot)
A technique in which proteins separated by SDS-PAGE are electrophoretically transferred to a membrane and detected with a specific primary antibody and an enzyme-linked secondary antibody.
Towbin et al., 1979; named by W. N. Burnette, 1981

Applications

  • Diagnosis of HIV: confirmatory test after a positive ELISA (detects antibodies to viral proteins).
  • Confirming expression of a recombinant protein.
  • Studying protein modification (phosphorylation) and degradation.
  • Detecting disease proteins such as prion protein (BSE).

Common problems

  • High background → insufficient blocking or too much antibody.
  • No band → poor transfer or inactive antibody.
  • Extra bands → degradation or cross-reactivity.

Related variants

  • Southwestern blot — proteins on a membrane probed with labelled DNA to find DNA-binding proteins.
  • Dot/slot blot — samples spotted directly without electrophoresis (presence/absence, no size).
  • Colony hybridisation — bacterial colonies lifted onto a membrane and probed to find recombinant clones.

📋 Summary

Summary & Exam Revision

Key points and model answers likely to appear in university examinations.

Key exam points
1
Southern blot (E. M. Southern, 1975) → DNA; Northern → RNA; Western → protein.
2
Gel → membrane transfer preserves the band pattern; probes then locate the target.
3
Southern and Northern blots rely on nucleic acid hybridisation; Western relies on antigen–antibody binding.
4
Southern: DNA is denatured with alkali. Northern: formaldehyde gel, no alkali, RNase-free.
5
Membranes: nylon/nitrocellulose for nucleic acids; nitrocellulose/PVDF for proteins.
6
Transfer: capillary (Southern/Northern) versus electroblotting (Western).
7
Blocking reduces background; stringency is raised by high temperature and low salt.
8
Uses: transgene confirmation, RFLP, DNA fingerprinting (Southern); gene expression (Northern); HIV confirmation, recombinant protein (Western).
Short-answer questions

Click a question to reveal the model answer.

Q.1What is blotting? Name the three types.▼

Blotting is the transfer of electrophoretically separated DNA, RNA or protein from a gel to a membrane, followed by detection with a labelled probe. The three types are Southern (DNA), Northern (RNA) and Western (protein).

Q.2Describe the steps of Southern blotting.▼

Restriction digestion of DNA → agarose gel electrophoresis → depurination and alkali denaturation → capillary transfer to nitrocellulose/nylon membrane → fixation by UV or baking → prehybridisation (blocking) → hybridisation with labelled probe → washing → autoradiographic detection.

Q.3How does Northern blotting differ from Southern blotting?▼

Northern detects RNA; the gel is denaturing (formaldehyde), alkali is not used because it hydrolyses RNA, and RNase-free conditions are essential. It shows transcript size and level of expression.

Q.4Explain the principle of Western blotting.▼

Proteins separated by SDS-PAGE are electroblotted onto a membrane, blocked, and probed with a specific primary antibody. An enzyme-linked secondary antibody binds the primary antibody and generates a visible or chemiluminescent signal at the position of the target protein.

Q.5What is stringency in hybridisation?▼

Stringency is the strictness of conditions that determine how closely a probe must match its target to remain bound. High temperature and low salt give high stringency and fewer false positives.

Q.6Write two applications of Western blotting.▼

(1) Confirmatory test for HIV infection; (2) confirming expression and size of a recombinant protein in a host cell.

📖 Glossary

Key Terms & Glossary

Important terms in simple language.

Blotting
Transfer of separated biomolecules from a gel to a membrane for detection.
Probe
A labelled single-stranded nucleic acid (or antibody) that binds a specific target.
Hybridisation
Base pairing between a probe and its complementary target sequence.
Stringency
How demanding the washing/hybridisation conditions are; high stringency requires near-perfect matches.
Capillary transfer
Moving molecules from gel to membrane by buffer drawn up through absorbent paper.
Electroblotting
Transfer of proteins out of a gel to a membrane using an electric field.
Blocking agent
Protein or DNA solution (milk, BSA, Denhardt's) that covers free membrane sites.
Primary antibody
Antibody that recognises the target protein.
Secondary antibody
Enzyme-conjugated antibody that recognises the primary antibody.
Autoradiography
Detection of radioactive signal using X-ray film.
PVDF
Polyvinylidene difluoride: a membrane that binds proteins strongly.
Housekeeping gene
A constitutively expressed gene (e.g. GAPDH) used as a loading control.
