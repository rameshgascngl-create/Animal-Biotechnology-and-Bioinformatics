Section 1

🧬 Introduction to Vectors in Genetic Engineering

Definition of a Vector

A vector is a DNA molecule used as a vehicle to carry and transfer a foreign DNA fragment into a host cell, where it can be replicated and/or expressed. The word "vector" literally means "carrier."

Importance in Recombinant DNA Technology

Vectors are indispensable tools in genetic engineering because a foreign gene fragment by itself cannot survive or replicate inside a host cell. It must be linked to a vector that carries the necessary signals for replication, selection, and expression.

Core Functions of a Vector:
Carries the gene of interest into a host cell → Enables autonomous replication → Provides selectable markers to identify successful transformants → May facilitate expression of the cloned gene.

General Role in Transfer and Replication of Foreign DNA

  • The foreign gene is cut with a restriction enzyme and ligated into the vector.
  • The resulting recombinant vector is introduced into a host cell (e.g., E. coli).
  • Inside the host, the vector replicates using its own origin of replication (ori), producing many copies of the foreign gene.
  • Cells carrying the recombinant vector are identified using selectable markers such as antibiotic resistance genes.
Section 2

⭕ Plasmids

Definition

Plasmids are small, circular, double-stranded, extrachromosomal DNA molecules found naturally in bacteria (and some yeasts and archaea). They replicate independently of the bacterial chromosome using their own origin of replication.

General Characteristics

  • Size: Typically 1 to 200 kilobase pairs (kb), much smaller than the chromosome.
  • Shape: Circular double-stranded DNA, usually supercoiled.
  • Replication: Autonomous replication; independent of the host chromosome.
  • Dispensable: Not essential for the host's survival under normal conditions.
  • Transmissible: Many plasmids can be transferred between bacteria by conjugation.
  • Copy number: Present in multiple copies per cell (from 1 to >100 copies, depending on the type).

Natural Occurrence in Bacteria

Plasmids are found naturally in a wide variety of bacteria, including Escherichia coli, Agrobacterium tumefaciens, Staphylococcus aureus, and many others. They often carry genes that provide selective advantages, such as antibiotic resistance or the ability to metabolize unusual compounds.

Importance in Biotechnology

  • Widely used as cloning vectors due to their small size and easy manipulation.
  • Can carry and replicate foreign DNA fragments in bacterial hosts.
  • Selectable markers on plasmids allow identification of transformed cells.
  • Form the basis for expression vectors used to produce recombinant proteins (e.g., insulin, growth hormone).
Section 3

🗂️ Types of Plasmids

Plasmids are classified based on the genes they carry and the functions they confer:

Fertility (F) Plasmids Carry tra (transfer) genes that enable conjugation and plasmid transfer between bacteria. The host cell becomes a donor (F⁺). Example: F plasmid of E. coli.
Resistance (R) Plasmids Carry genes for resistance to one or more antibiotics or heavy metals. Major cause of multi-drug resistance in pathogens. Example: pBR322 (carries amp and tet resistance genes).
Col Plasmids Carry genes encoding colicins — bacteriocins (toxic proteins) that kill other bacteria lacking the same plasmid. Example: ColE1 plasmid of E. coli.
Degradative Plasmids Carry genes encoding enzymes for breakdown of unusual organic compounds such as toluene, camphor, and salicylate. Example: TOL plasmid of Pseudomonas putida.
Virulence Plasmids Carry genes that enable bacteria to infect and cause disease in host organisms. Example: Ti plasmid of Agrobacterium tumefaciens (causes crown gall disease in plants).
Section 4

⭐ Characteristic Features of an Ideal Vector

An ideal cloning vector should possess the following essential characteristics:

Origin of Replication (ori)

A specific DNA sequence where replication begins. Allows the vector to replicate autonomously inside the host cell, independent of the host chromosome.

Selectable Marker Genes

Genes (e.g., antibiotic resistance genes) that allow identification and selection of transformed host cells carrying the recombinant vector.

Unique Restriction Sites / MCS

A multiple cloning site (MCS) or polylinker with multiple unique restriction enzyme sites for convenient insertion of foreign DNA fragments.

Small Size

Small vectors are easier to introduce into host cells (transformation efficiency), manipulate in the lab, and less likely to be degraded inside the host.

High Copy Number

Producing many copies per host cell ensures an abundant supply of the cloned DNA for analysis, sequencing, and expression studies.

Stability Inside Host

The vector must be stably maintained inside the host cell and not be lost or degraded during cell division across multiple generations.

Easy Isolation

The vector (and its recombinant form) must be easily isolated from host cell lysates and purified using standard laboratory procedures.

Memory Aid (ORSSCSE): Origin of replication, Restriction sites, Selectable markers, Small size, Copy number high, Stability, Easy isolation.
Section 5

🔵 Plasmid Vectors

A. pBR322 — The Classic Cloning Vector

pBR322 is one of the most widely used and historically important E. coli plasmid cloning vectors. It was constructed in 1977 by Bolivar, Rodriguez, and colleagues (hence the name — p for plasmid, BR for Bolivar and Rodriguez, and 322 as the strain number).

Key Properties at a Glance:
Size: 4,361 bp  |  Host: E. coli  |  Copy number: ~15–20 copies per cell  |  Two antibiotic resistance markers

Important Features of pBR322

  • Origin of replication (ori): Derived from pMB1 (a ColE1-type replicon); enables autonomous replication in E. coli.
  • Ampicillin resistance gene (ampr): Confers resistance to ampicillin; used to select transformed cells.
  • Tetracycline resistance gene (tetr): Confers resistance to tetracycline; used for insertional inactivation screening.
  • Multiple restriction sites: Contains unique sites for EcoRI, HindIII, BamHI, SalI, ClaI, PstI, and others within or near the resistance genes.
  • Small size: At 4,361 bp, it is easily isolated and transformed into E. coli.

Insertional Inactivation — Cloning Strategy

If a foreign DNA fragment is inserted into the BamHI or SalI site (within the tetr gene), the tetracycline resistance gene is disrupted. Transformed cells can be screened:

  • Cells on ampicillin medium → grow (carry the vector)
  • Same cells on tetracycline medium → do NOT grow (tetr disrupted by insert = recombinant)
  • This strategy is called insertional inactivation.
Insertion into the PstI site (within ampr) disrupts ampicillin resistance instead. Recombinants will be amp-sensitive but tet-resistant.

B. Ti Plasmid — The Plant Engineer

The Ti plasmid (Tumour-inducing plasmid) is a naturally occurring virulence plasmid found in Agrobacterium tumefaciens, a gram-negative soil bacterium that causes crown gall disease in dicotyledonous plants.

Structure and Key Regions

  • T-DNA (Transfer DNA): A ~25 kb segment of the Ti plasmid that is transferred and stably integrated into the plant nuclear genome. Contains onc genes (for opine synthesis and tumour formation).
  • vir region: ~35 kb of virulence genes (virA, virB, virC, virD, virE, virG) that mediate T-DNA transfer into the plant cell.
  • ori region: Origin of replication for maintenance in Agrobacterium.
  • Opine catabolism genes: Enable Agrobacterium to metabolize opines (amino acid-sugar compounds) produced by the infected plant.

Use in Plant Genetic Engineering

To use the Ti plasmid as a vector, the tumour-causing genes in T-DNA are disarmed (removed) and replaced with the gene of interest. The modified Ti plasmid (called a binary vector system) is used to deliver foreign genes into plant cells, where they integrate into the plant genome and are stably inherited.

Key Significance: The Ti plasmid is the most important vector system for creating transgenic plants — including herbicide-resistant, pest-resistant, and nutritionally enhanced crop plants (e.g., Golden Rice).
Section 6

🦠 Bacteriophage Vectors

A. Lambda (λ) Phage — The Genomic Library Builder

Lambda phage (bacteriophage λ) is a temperate bacteriophage that infects E. coli. It was one of the earliest and most powerful cloning vectors developed for genetic engineering.

Structure and Basic Properties

  • Genome: Linear double-stranded DNA, approximately 48.5 kb in length.
  • Sticky ends: Twelve-nucleotide single-stranded cos sites (complementary overhangs) at each end; these allow the linear DNA to circularize inside the host.
  • Two life cycles: Lytic cycle (replication and cell lysis) and lysogenic cycle (integration into host chromosome as a prophage).
  • Structure: Icosahedral head (containing the DNA), a tail, and tail fibers for host recognition.

Use as a Cloning Vector

The central one-third of lambda's genome (~15 kb, containing non-essential genes for lysogeny) can be replaced by foreign DNA. Two types of lambda vectors are used:

  • Insertion vectors: Foreign DNA is inserted at a single restriction site. Accommodate up to ~8 kb of insert.
  • Replacement vectors: The non-essential "stuffer" fragment is replaced by foreign DNA. Accommodate up to ~20–23 kb of insert.
Insert capacity: Up to 23 kb (replacement vectors)  |  Key advantage: Much larger insert capacity than plasmids (typically <10 kb).

Importance in Gene Libraries

Lambda phage vectors are ideal for constructing genomic DNA libraries because they can accommodate large inserts. The recombinant phage particles are packaged in vitro and can infect E. coli cells with very high efficiency (10⁸–10⁹ plaques per microgram DNA).

B. M13 Phage — The Sequencing Vector

M13 is a filamentous bacteriophage that specifically infects male (F⁺) strains of E. coli via the F pilus. It is uniquely suited for applications requiring single-stranded DNA.

Structure and Basic Properties

  • Genome: Circular, single-stranded DNA (ssDNA), approximately 6,407 nucleotides.
  • Shape: Filamentous (rod-shaped, ~900 nm long × 6 nm wide) — not icosahedral.
  • Non-lytic: M13 does not lyse its host; phage particles are continuously secreted through the cell membrane, allowing the host to survive.
  • Replication: Replicates as double-stranded replicative form (RF) inside the host; but packages single-stranded DNA into phage particles.

Use in Cloning and DNA Sequencing

  • The RF (double-stranded) form can be manipulated like a plasmid — cut with restriction enzymes and ligated with insert DNA.
  • After replication, single-stranded DNA is packaged into phage particles, providing a ready source of ssDNA for Sanger DNA sequencing.
  • Single-stranded DNA is also used as a template for site-directed mutagenesis.
  • Insert capacity is limited (up to ~1.5 kb for stable maintenance), but the biological production of ssDNA makes M13 uniquely valuable.
Key Significance: M13-based vectors (mp series, e.g., M13mp18, M13mp19) were essential for the development of chain-termination (Sanger) DNA sequencing and phage display technology.

Section 13

📖 Glossary of Key Terms

Vector
A DNA molecule (plasmid, phage, or artificial chromosome) used to carry and transfer foreign DNA into a host cell where it can replicate and/or be expressed.
Origin of Replication (ori)
A specific DNA sequence on a vector where replication is initiated. Ensures the vector can replicate autonomously inside the host cell, independent of the chromosomal DNA.
Selectable Marker
A gene (typically conferring antibiotic resistance) on a vector that allows selection of transformed host cells on selective media. Untransformed cells die; transformed cells survive.
Multiple Cloning Site (MCS)
A short DNA sequence on a cloning vector that contains multiple unique restriction enzyme recognition sites. Also called a polylinker; facilitates insertion of foreign DNA at a defined location.
Insert
The foreign DNA fragment (gene of interest) that is cloned into the vector. After restriction digestion and ligation, the insert becomes part of the recombinant DNA molecule.
T-DNA
Transfer DNA — the ~25 kb segment of the Ti plasmid that is excised and transferred by Agrobacterium into the plant cell, where it integrates stably into the plant nuclear genome.
Phage / Bacteriophage
A virus that specifically infects bacteria. Used as a cloning vector because it can inject its DNA (along with foreign inserts) into bacterial host cells with high efficiency.
Host Cell
The cell (usually E. coli or plant cells) into which the recombinant vector is introduced. The host provides the cellular machinery needed for vector replication and gene expression.
Insertional Inactivation
A screening method in which insertion of foreign DNA into a restriction site within a marker gene disrupts its function, allowing identification of recombinant clones. Used in pBR322 cloning.
cos Site
Cohesive-end (cos) sites on the lambda phage genome — 12-nt single-stranded overhangs at both ends of the linear DNA. Essential for circularization inside the host and phage DNA packaging.
Cloning
The process of inserting a DNA fragment into a vector, introducing it into a host cell, and propagating it to produce many identical copies of the DNA fragment.
Transformation
The process of introducing recombinant plasmid DNA into a competent bacterial host cell. Successfully transformed cells carry the recombinant vector and are identified by selection on antibiotic media.
