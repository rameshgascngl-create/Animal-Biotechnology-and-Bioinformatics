PCR: Polymerase Chain Reaction

Principle  ·  Components  ·  Types  ·  Applications

Imagine making a million copies of a single gene in just a few hours — without any living cells. PCR made that possible. This revolutionary technique, invented by Kary Mullis in 1983 (Nobel Prize 1993), is the backbone of modern molecular biology, diagnostics, forensics, and genetic research.

Section 1

🔬 Introduction to PCR

Full Form and Definition

PCR stands for Polymerase Chain Reaction. It is an in vitro technique for rapidly amplifying (making millions of copies of) a specific DNA segment from a small amount of starting material, using repeated cycles of heating and cooling.

History

PCR was invented by Kary Mullis in 1983 while working at Cetus Corporation. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1993 for this revolutionary discovery. The use of Taq DNA polymerase (from the thermophilic bacterium Thermus aquaticus) — which is heat-stable — made PCR practical for automation.

Importance in Molecular Biology and Biotechnology

  • Amplification from trace amounts: Can amplify DNA from a single molecule to billions of copies.
  • Universal tool: Foundation for DNA sequencing, cloning, genotyping, diagnosis, and forensics.
  • Speed: A complete PCR run takes only 1–3 hours.
  • Sensitivity and specificity: Detects a single pathogen DNA in a complex sample.
  • No cell culture required: Works on any DNA source — blood, tissue, bone, bacteria, viruses.

Basic Concept of DNA Amplification

PCR mimics the natural process of DNA replication — but in a test tube. A short segment of double-stranded DNA is separated into two single strands (denaturation), short synthetic DNA sequences (primers) bind to each strand (annealing), and a heat-stable DNA polymerase copies each strand (extension). Each cycle doubles the number of target DNA copies — leading to exponential amplification: 2ⁿ copies after n cycles.

Key Fact: After just 30 cycles, a single DNA molecule can theoretically produce over 1 billion (2³⁰ ≈ 10⁹) copies of the target sequence.
Section 2

⚗️ Principle of PCR

Basic Principle

PCR is based on the principle of enzymatic in vitro DNA replication. A specific segment of DNA is amplified using repeated cycles of three temperature-dependent steps: denaturation, annealing, and extension. The use of a thermostable DNA polymerase (Taq polymerase) allows the reaction to be automated in a thermal cycler.

Role of Complementary Base Pairing

Two short oligonucleotide primers are designed to be complementary to sequences flanking the target DNA segment — one primer for each strand. According to Watson-Crick base pairing (A–T and G–C), primers bind specifically to their complementary sequences at reduced temperatures. This specificity ensures that only the target region between the two primers is amplified.

Role of Repeated Thermal Cycling

A single cycle produces only two copies from one template. However, because the products of each cycle also serve as templates for subsequent cycles, the amount of target DNA doubles with every cycle. A thermal cycler automatically controls the heating and cooling between the three stages.

Exponential Amplification

Formula: Number of copies = 2ⁿ (where n = number of cycles)
After 10 cycles → 1,024 copies  |  After 20 cycles → 1,048,576 copies  |  After 30 cycles → 1,073,741,824 (~10⁹) copies
Section 3

🧪 Components Required for PCR

1. Template DNA
The source DNA containing the target sequence to be amplified. Can be genomic DNA, cDNA, or plasmid DNA. Usually in very small amounts (nanogram quantities).
2. Primers (×2)
Short synthetic oligonucleotides (18–25 nt) complementary to sequences flanking the target region. Two primers: forward (sense) and reverse (antisense), one for each strand. Define the exact region to be amplified.
3. Taq DNA Polymerase
A heat-stable DNA polymerase from Thermus aquaticus. Withstands 95°C denaturation cycles. Synthesises the new strand in the 5'→3' direction. Optimal activity at ~72°C.
4. dNTPs (all four)
Deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates: dATP, dTTP, dGTP, dCTP. The building blocks for new DNA strand synthesis. Typically added at 200 µM each.
5. Buffer
Typically Tris-HCl (pH 8.3–8.8) — maintains optimal pH for Taq polymerase activity. Includes KCl to stabilise primer–template interactions.
6. Mg²⁺ Ions
Magnesium chloride (MgCl₂) at 1.5–2.5 mM. Essential cofactor for Taq DNA polymerase activity. Also stabilises primer–template duplexes. Concentration affects specificity — excess Mg²⁺ increases non-specific amplification.
7. Thermal Cycler
A programmable device that precisely controls and rapidly alternates between the three reaction temperatures (95°C, 50–65°C, 72°C) for the required number of cycles. Enables complete automation of PCR.
Typical PCR Reaction Volume: 25–50 µL in a microcentrifuge tube or 96-well plate. The reaction is set up on ice to prevent premature DNA synthesis.
Section 4

🌡️ Steps / Stages of Each PCR Cycle

Each PCR cycle consists of three precisely temperature-controlled stages:

1
94–96°C Denaturation

The double-stranded template DNA is heated to 94–96°C. At this temperature, the hydrogen bonds holding the two complementary strands together are broken, separating the DNA into two single-stranded templates. This process is called denaturation. Duration: 20–30 seconds per cycle (initial denaturation: 2–5 minutes to ensure complete denaturation).

  • This step is critical — incomplete denaturation leads to poor amplification.
  • Taq polymerase is heat-stable and survives this temperature.
2
50–65°C Annealing

The temperature is lowered to 50–65°C (varies by primer sequence). The two primers bind to their complementary sequences on the single-stranded template DNA through hydrogen bonding. This is called primer annealing. Duration: 20–40 seconds.

  • The annealing temperature is usually 5°C below the melting temperature (Tm) of the primers.
  • Too low: non-specific annealing → non-specific bands. Too high: no annealing → no product.
  • The forward primer anneals to the antisense strand; the reverse primer anneals to the sense strand.
3
72°C Extension / Elongation

The temperature is raised to 72°C — the optimal working temperature of Taq DNA polymerase. Starting from the 3'-OH of each annealed primer, Taq polymerase adds dNTPs complementary to the template in the 5'→3' direction. This is called extension or elongation. Duration: ~1 minute per 1 kb of target.

  • Taq polymerase extends at ~1000 bp/minute at 72°C.
  • A final extension step (5–10 minutes at 72°C) completes any partially synthesised strands.
  • Result: two copies of the target DNA from each original template.
Number of Cycles: Typically 25–40 cycles. After the last cycle, a final extension at 72°C for 5–10 minutes ensures all incomplete strands are finished. Reactions are then held at 4°C for storage.

Section 7

🗂️ Types of PCR

1. Conventional PCR Principle: Standard thermal cycling with two primers amplifying a specific DNA region.
Detection: Agarose gel electrophoresis after the reaction.
Use: Basic amplification, clone verification, pathogen detection.
Limitation: Qualitative — shows presence/absence, not quantity.
2. RT-PCR (Reverse Transcriptase PCR) Principle: mRNA is first converted to cDNA using reverse transcriptase, then the cDNA is amplified by standard PCR.
Use: Detecting gene expression; diagnosing RNA viruses (e.g., SARS-CoV-2, HIV, Influenza).
Application: COVID-19 diagnostic tests (RT-PCR).
3. Real-Time PCR / qPCR Principle: Amplification is monitored in real time using fluorescent dyes (SYBR Green) or probes (TaqMan). Fluorescence increases as product accumulates.
Use: Quantification of gene expression, viral load, GMO detection.
Advantage: Quantitative — measures the amount of starting DNA/RNA.
4. Multiplex PCR Principle: Multiple primer pairs used in a single reaction to amplify several target sequences simultaneously.
Use: Detecting multiple pathogens at once; STR profiling in forensics; deletion/duplication screening in genetic disorders.
Advantage: Time-saving, cost-effective for multiple targets.
5. Nested PCR Principle: Two successive PCR reactions. The second PCR uses an inner (nested) pair of primers within the product of the first PCR.
Use: Detecting very low copy number targets (e.g., HIV in early infection, rare mutations).
Advantage: Extremely high sensitivity and specificity.
Additional Types (brief): LAMP-PCR (Loop-mediated isothermal amplification), Hot-Start PCR (minimises non-specific amplification), Digital PCR (absolute quantification), RACE-PCR (rapid amplification of cDNA ends), Allele-Specific PCR (detects single nucleotide variations).
Section 8

🏥 Applications of PCR

Medical Diagnosis
Rapid, sensitive detection of bacterial and viral infections. RT-PCR diagnoses SARS-CoV-2, HIV, hepatitis B & C, tuberculosis (Mycobacterium tuberculosis), and sexually transmitted infections.
Pathogen Detection
Identifies pathogens in food (Salmonella, E. coli O157:H7), water supplies, and environmental samples. Detects organisms that cannot be easily cultured.
Genetic Disorder Analysis
Detects mutations in genes causing cystic fibrosis, sickle cell anaemia, Huntington's disease, BRCA1/2 (breast cancer). Prenatal diagnosis from foetal cells in amniotic fluid.
Forensic Science
DNA profiling from trace evidence — blood, hair, skin cells at crime scenes. STR (short tandem repeat) analysis using multiplex PCR. Identification of disaster victims.
DNA Fingerprinting
PCR amplifies specific STR loci unique to each individual. Used in paternity testing, immigration cases, and individual identification in forensics.
Gene Cloning
Amplifies a gene of interest before cloning into a vector. Confirms successful gene insertion by colony PCR. Generates insert DNA for sequencing.
Animal Biotechnology
Sex determination of embryos in livestock breeding. Detection of transgene integration in transgenic animals. Genetic characterisation of breeds and species identification.
Evolutionary & Research Studies
Ancient DNA analysis from museum specimens and fossils. Phylogenetic studies comparing species. Metagenomics — identifying microbial communities in environmental samples.
Section 9

⚖️ Advantages and Limitations

✅ Advantages

  • Extreme sensitivity: Detects a single DNA molecule from a complex sample.
  • High speed: Complete PCR in 1–3 hours.
  • Versatility: Works on any DNA source — blood, bone, hair, formalin-fixed tissue.
  • High specificity: Primer-specific amplification of exact target sequence.
  • Small sample requirement: Nanogram or even picogram quantities of DNA.
  • Automation: Thermal cyclers enable high-throughput, standardised results.
  • No radioactivity required (fluorescent methods).

⚠️ Limitations

  • Prior sequence knowledge required: Primers must be designed from known sequence.
  • Contamination: Extreme sensitivity means even trace contamination gives false positives.
  • Non-specific amplification: Incorrect primer binding (mispriming) produces spurious bands if conditions are suboptimal.
  • Taq polymerase error rate: Taq lacks proofreading (3'→5' exonuclease) → introduces errors (~1 in 10⁴–10⁵ bp).
  • Length limitation: Standard PCR reliably amplifies up to ~3–5 kb; long-range PCR required for larger fragments.
  • Qualitative (conventional): Standard PCR shows presence/absence — not quantity without qPCR.
Common Causes of PCR Failure: (1) Contamination with exogenous DNA → false positive; (2) Primer dimers (primers bind each other) → smear or small non-specific band; (3) Low Mg²⁺ → no amplification; high Mg²⁺ → non-specific bands; (4) Poor DNA template quality (degraded or inhibitors present).

Section 15

📖 Glossary

PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction)
An in vitro technique for amplifying a specific DNA segment exponentially using repeated thermal cycling steps (denaturation, annealing, extension) and a thermostable DNA polymerase. Invented by Kary Mullis in 1983.
Template DNA
The source DNA containing the target sequence to be amplified in PCR. Can be genomic DNA, cDNA, plasmid DNA, or forensic/clinical samples. Typically used in nanogram quantities per reaction.
Primer
A short (18–25 nt) synthetic single-stranded oligonucleotide complementary to the template flanking the target region. Two primers are required: forward (sense) and reverse (antisense). Defines the exact boundaries of the amplified product (amplicon).
Denaturation
The first stage of each PCR cycle. Heating to 94–96°C breaks hydrogen bonds between base pairs, separating the double-stranded DNA into single-stranded templates for primer binding in the next step.
Annealing
The second stage of each PCR cycle (50–65°C). Primers hybridise to their complementary sequences on the single-stranded template through Watson-Crick base pairing. Annealing temperature = Tm − 5°C of the primers.
Extension
The third stage of each PCR cycle (72°C). Taq DNA polymerase extends each primer by adding dNTPs complementary to the template in the 5'→3' direction, synthesising a new complementary DNA strand.
Taq Polymerase
A thermostable DNA polymerase isolated from Thermus aquaticus, a bacterium from hot springs. Withstands the 94–96°C denaturation step of PCR. Optimal activity at 72°C. Rate: ~1000 bp/min. Lacks 3'→5' proofreading exonuclease activity.
Thermal Cycler
A programmable device that automatically and precisely controls the temperature cycling of PCR reactions. Alternates between the denaturation, annealing, and extension temperatures for the programmed number of cycles, enabling complete automation of PCR.
Amplicon
The PCR product — the double-stranded DNA fragment amplified between (and including) the two primer sequences. Its size is fixed by the primer positions and is detected as a band on agarose gel electrophoresis.
Reverse Transcriptase
RNA-dependent DNA polymerase from retroviruses (e.g., MMLV-RT). In RT-PCR, converts single-stranded mRNA into complementary DNA (cDNA) before PCR amplification. Essential for detecting and studying RNA targets including RNA viruses.
