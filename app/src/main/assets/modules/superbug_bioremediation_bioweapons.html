Superbug, Bioremediation and Bioweapons

Engineered microbes can digest oil, but the same biology can be misused. This module explains the Chakrabarty superbug, the principles of bioremediation and the history, law and defence concerning biological weapons.

📗 Chapter 1

Superbug: The Oil-Eating Bacterium

The first genetically engineered organism to be patented was a "superbug" designed to degrade crude oil.

Superbug (in biotechnology)
A genetically engineered bacterium, developed by Ananda Mohan Chakrabarty (General Electric, 1971–72), carrying several hydrocarbon-degrading plasmids so that it can digest more components of crude oil than any single natural strain.
How the superbug was made

Different Pseudomonas strains each degrade only some hydrocarbons, because the genes sit on separate plasmids. Chakrabarty used conjugation to bring the plasmids into one cell.

PlasmidHydrocarbons degraded
CAMCamphor
OCTOctane (aliphatic hydrocarbons)
XYLXylene and toluene
NAHNaphthalene

Result

One bacterium (Pseudomonas sp., usually cited as P. putida) that degrades about two-thirds of the hydrocarbons in crude oil, faster than mixed natural strains. The CAM and OCT plasmids are incompatible and recombine into one hybrid plasmid in the engineered strain.

Diamond v. Chakrabarty (1980)
1971 — Patent application filed for the bacterium
1980 — US Supreme Court (5–4) rules that a live, human-made micro-organism is patentable subject matter
Impact — Opened the way to patenting biotechnology inventions, including later patents on transgenic organisms

Two different meanings of "superbug"

  • Biotechnology: Chakrabarty's oil-eating Pseudomonas.
  • Medicine: bacteria resistant to many antibiotics, e.g. MRSA, NDM-1-producing Enterobacteriaceae.
  • Read the exam question to see which sense is meant.

Why was it not widely used?

The strain was not adopted for large-scale clean-up. Engineered strains often compete poorly with native microbes, the strain degraded only some oil fractions, and release of genetically modified organisms is tightly regulated.

📘 Chapter 2

Bioremediation

Living organisms are used to break down or immobilise pollutants and restore a contaminated site.

Bioremediation
The use of micro-organisms, fungi, plants or their enzymes to degrade, detoxify or remove pollutants from soil, water or air, converting them to less harmful products (ideally CO2, water and biomass).
Approaches
🌿

Biostimulation

Adding nutrients (N, P), oxygen or electron acceptors to stimulate the native pollutant-degrading microbes.

🦠

Bioaugmentation

Adding specially selected or engineered microbes to a site that lacks degraders.

🌻

Phytoremediation

Using plants to extract, stabilise or degrade contaminants (e.g. sunflower, Indian mustard for heavy metals).

🍄

Mycoremediation

Using fungi, e.g. white-rot fungi that degrade lignin-like pollutants and dyes.

In situ versus ex situ
In situEx situ
WhereAt the contaminated siteMaterial excavated and treated elsewhere
ExamplesBioventing, biosparging, natural attenuationLand farming, biopiles, bioreactors
CostLower, less disturbanceHigher, but better control
SpeedSlowerFaster

Case study: Exxon Valdez (Alaska, 1989)

About 11 million US gallons of crude oil polluted Prince William Sound shorelines. Fertiliser was applied to stimulate native oil-degrading bacteria (biostimulation), which greatly accelerated oil removal on treated beaches. It is the classic example of large-scale bioremediation.

Advantages and limits

  • Advantages: low cost, eco-friendly, converts pollutants to harmless products, minimal disturbance.
  • Limits: slow, site-specific, incomplete for some pollutants (e.g. some heavy metals cannot be degraded, only transformed), and release of engineered strains is regulated.

📙 Chapter 3

Bioweapons and Biosecurity

Biological agents can be misused to cause disease. Understanding the history, classification and legal controls is part of responsible biotechnology.

Biological weapon
A biological weapon uses pathogens (bacteria, viruses, fungi) or their toxins to cause disease or death in humans, animals or plants. Bioterrorism is the deliberate release of such agents by non-state actors.

This chapter is limited to the general, educational level required by the syllabus: classification, history, international law and defence.

CDC categories of bioterrorism agents
CategoryDescriptionExamples
AHighest risk: easily spread, high mortality, need special public-health preparednessAnthrax (Bacillus anthracis), smallpox (variola virus), plague (Yersinia pestis), botulism toxin, tularaemia, viral haemorrhagic fevers
BModerately easy to spread, lower mortalityBrucellosis, Q fever, ricin toxin, salmonellosis
CEmerging pathogens that could be engineeredNipah virus, hantavirus
History and landmarks
1925 — Geneva Protocol prohibits use of chemical and biological weapons in war
1972 / 1975 — Biological Weapons Convention (BWC) opened for signature in 1972; in force from 1975; bans development, production and stockpiling
1979 — Accidental anthrax release at Sverdlovsk (USSR) killed dozens of people
1995 — Aum Shinrikyo sarin attack in Tokyo; the cult had also attempted biological attacks
2001 — Anthrax-contaminated letters in the USA (5 deaths)

Defence and prevention

  • Surveillance and rapid diagnostics (PCR, immunoassays).
  • Vaccines and stockpiled antibiotics/antitoxins.
  • Biosafety levels BSL-1 to BSL-4 in laboratories.
  • Dual-use research of concern oversight — research that has legitimate use but could be misused.
  • Export controls on dangerous pathogens (Australia Group).

Relevance to India

India is a party to the Biological Weapons Convention (ratified in 1974) and regulates dangerous pathogens through the Environment (Protection) Act 1986, the Rules for hazardous micro-organisms and genetically engineered organisms (1989), and the WMD Act, 2005.

Short-answer questions

Click a question to reveal the model answer.

Q.1What is a superbug? Who developed it?▼

A genetically engineered Pseudomonas carrying plasmids (CAM, OCT, XYL, NAH) for degrading different hydrocarbons, developed by A. M. Chakrabarty (General Electric) and patented after the US Supreme Court decision of 1980.

Q.2Define bioremediation. Distinguish biostimulation and bioaugmentation.▼

Bioremediation is the use of living organisms to degrade or detoxify pollutants. Biostimulation adds nutrients/oxygen to stimulate native microbes; bioaugmentation adds specialised microbes.

Q.3Differentiate in situ and ex situ bioremediation.▼

In situ treats pollutants at the site (bioventing, biosparging); ex situ treats excavated material elsewhere (land farming, biopiles, bioreactors).

Q.4What are bioweapons? Name two agents.▼

Bioweapons use pathogens or toxins to cause disease. Examples: Bacillus anthracis (anthrax), Yersinia pestis (plague), variola virus, botulinum toxin.

Q.5Name the international treaty on biological weapons.▼

The Biological Weapons Convention (opened 1972; in force 1975) prohibits development, production and stockpiling of biological and toxin weapons.

📖 Glossary

Key Terms & Glossary

Important terms in simple language.

Superbug
Engineered oil-degrading bacterium; also a drug-resistant pathogen in medicine.
Bioremediation
Use of organisms to clean polluted environments.
Biostimulation
Stimulating native microbes by adding nutrients or oxygen.
Bioaugmentation
Adding specialised microbes to a polluted site.
Phytoremediation
Using plants to remove or degrade pollutants.
Xenobiotic
Foreign chemical not normally found in nature, e.g. pesticide.
Biosparging
Injecting air below the water table to stimulate aerobic degradation.
Bioterrorism
Deliberate release of pathogens or toxins to harm populations.
BWC
Biological Weapons Convention (in force 1975).
Biosafety level
Containment level (BSL-1 to BSL-4) matching the risk of the agent.
