Section 1

🔬 Introduction to Advanced Vectors

What Is a Vector?

A vector is a DNA molecule used to carry and deliver a foreign gene into a host cell, where it can be replicated and/or expressed. Simple vectors such as plasmids and bacteriophage lambda were developed first and remain important tools. However, they have significant limitations that led to the development of advanced vectors.

Limitations of Simple Plasmid Vectors

  • Small insert capacity: Plasmids typically accept only up to 5–10 kb of foreign DNA efficiently; large genes or genomic regions cannot be cloned.
  • Prokaryotic host only: Standard plasmids replicate in bacteria and cannot function in eukaryotic (animal or yeast) host cells.
  • No integration: Plasmids replicate episomally (separate from the chromosome) and can be lost during cell division; stable integration requires additional tools.
  • Limited genome analysis: Small insert sizes make constructing complete genomic libraries of large genomes impractical.

Need for Advanced Vectors

Advanced vectors were developed to: carry very large DNA inserts (up to several hundred kb), function in eukaryotic hosts (yeast, animal cells, plants), integrate stably into the host genome, and enable the cloning of entire genes including their regulatory regions.
Cosmid

Hybrid of plasmid + lambda cos sites. Accepts 35–45 kb inserts. Replicates in bacteria.

YAC

Yeast Artificial Chromosome. Accepts 200–2000 kb inserts. Replicates in yeast cells.

SV40

Animal virus vector. Delivers genes into mammalian cells. Used in gene expression studies.

Transposon

Mobile genetic elements. Insert themselves into chromosomes. Used for insertional mutagenesis and gene delivery.

Section 2

⭕ Cosmid Vectors

Definition

A cosmid is a hybrid cloning vector that combines features of a plasmid with the cos sites of bacteriophage lambda. The name comes from cos (cohesive end site) + plasmid. Cosmids were developed by Collins and Hohn in 1978.

Structure of a Cosmid

  • Plasmid backbone: Contains an origin of replication (ori) for autonomous replication in E. coli, and at least one selectable marker (e.g., antibiotic resistance gene).
  • cos sites: The 12-nucleotide cohesive end sequences from lambda phage are incorporated into the plasmid. These are essential for in vitro packaging into lambda phage heads.
  • Restriction cloning sites (MCS): Multiple cloning site for insertion of large foreign DNA fragments.
  • Size of cosmid vector itself: Only ~5–6 kb (small backbone), which is why it can accommodate very large inserts.

Key Features

Insert Capacity

35–45 kb of foreign DNA — far larger than ordinary plasmids (~5–10 kb) or lambda phage (~23 kb).

In Vitro Packaging

Cosmid recombinants are packaged into lambda phage heads in vitro and introduced into E. coli by phage transduction — very high efficiency.

Replication

Replicates like a plasmid inside E. coli using the ori; not dependent on phage replication machinery.

Selection

Transformed bacteria selected by antibiotic resistance markers carried on the plasmid backbone.

How Cosmid Packaging Works

  1. Large foreign DNA is cut with a restriction enzyme and ligated into the cosmid vector.
  2. The recombinant cosmid (35–45 kb total) is packaged in vitro into lambda phage head proteins (which require DNA between two cos sites, typically 37–52 kb in size).
  3. The packaged cosmid is used to infect E. coli cells — entering via lambda's efficient injection mechanism.
  4. Inside the cell, the cos sites are cleaved, and the cosmid circularizes and replicates as a plasmid.

Advantages

  • Very large insert capacity (35–45 kb) — ideal for cloning whole genes including introns and regulatory regions.
  • High transformation efficiency due to phage-mediated delivery.
  • Stable plasmid replication in E. coli.
  • Widely used to construct genomic libraries of complex organisms.

Limitations

  • Cannot accommodate inserts larger than ~45 kb (packaging size constraint).
  • Works only in bacterial (E. coli) host cells.
  • Chimeric clones (multiple ligated fragments) can be a problem.
Application: Cosmids were used extensively in the Human Genome Project to construct genomic libraries and generate sequence-ready contigs (overlapping clone maps).
Section 3

🧫 Yeast Artificial Chromosome (YAC)

Definition

A Yeast Artificial Chromosome (YAC) is a vector constructed to behave like a functional eukaryotic chromosome inside Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baker's yeast). It can accept and stably maintain enormous fragments of foreign DNA — from 200 kb to 2 Mb (2,000 kb). YACs were developed by Burke, Carle, and Olson in 1987.

Structure of a YAC

A functional YAC must contain all the essential elements of a eukaryotic chromosome:

  • Telomeres (TEL): Repetitive DNA sequences at both ends of the linear YAC. Prevent chromosome degradation and are essential for linear chromosome stability. Derived from Tetrahymena or yeast telomeric sequences.
  • Centromere (CEN): A sequence required for proper chromosome segregation during cell division (mitosis and meiosis). Ensures the YAC is distributed equally to daughter cells.
  • Origin of replication (ARS — Autonomously Replicating Sequence): Allows the YAC to replicate once per cell cycle in synchrony with the yeast chromosomes.
  • Selectable markers: Typically yeast nutritional markers (e.g., TRP1 and URA3) for selection in yeast, and a bacterial marker for propagation in E. coli.
  • Restriction cloning sites: For insertion of the large foreign DNA fragment.
Key Formula: A YAC = TEL + CEN + ARS + Selectable Markers + Cloning Site + TEL (plus the large foreign DNA insert between the two arms)

How a YAC Is Constructed

  1. The YAC vector (linear or circular) is digested with restriction enzymes to generate left arm (TEL + CEN + ARS + marker) and right arm (TEL + marker).
  2. Large foreign DNA (200 kb–2 Mb) is inserted between the two arms using ligation.
  3. The recombinant YAC is introduced into yeast spheroplasts by transformation (polyethylene glycol/calcium chloride method).
  4. Successful transformants are selected on selective media lacking the amino acids corresponding to the marker genes.

Features and Properties

Insert Capacity

200 kb to 2,000 kb (2 Mb). Largest capacity of all standard cloning vectors.

Host

Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baker's yeast). A simple eukaryote that can propagate in the lab.

Segregation

CEN ensures accurate distribution to daughter cells during mitosis — behaves like a real chromosome.

Instability

YACs can suffer from chimeric inserts (two unrelated fragments joined) and deletion of internal sequences — major limitation.

Applications

  • Human Genome Project: YAC libraries covered the entire human genome; used for physical mapping and sequencing.
  • Cloning very large genes: Genes spanning hundreds of kilobases (e.g., dystrophin at 2.4 Mb) can only be cloned in YACs.
  • Chromosome walking: Identifying overlapping YAC clones to navigate across chromosomal regions.
  • Positional cloning: Mapping and isolating disease genes by their chromosomal location.
Limitation: YAC inserts are often chimeric (~50% of clones contain DNA from two non-contiguous regions) and unstable. BACs (Bacterial Artificial Chromosomes) and PACs later replaced YACs in genome sequencing projects due to greater stability.
Section 4

🦠 Animal Vector – SV40 (Simian Virus 40)

Definition

SV40 (Simian Virus 40) is a small double-stranded circular DNA virus that naturally infects African green monkeys and can also infect certain human cells. It was one of the first animal viruses used as a cloning vector for introducing foreign genes into mammalian cells.

Source and Discovery

SV40 was discovered in 1960 as a contaminant of early poliovirus vaccine preparations grown in rhesus monkey kidney cells. It became a crucial research tool in molecular biology for studying DNA replication, transcription, and cell transformation in animal cells.

Structure and Genome Features

  • Genome size: ~5,243 base pairs of circular double-stranded DNA.
  • Capsid: Non-enveloped icosahedral protein shell (45 nm diameter).
  • Early region: Encodes Large T antigen (LT-Ag) and Small T antigen. LT-Ag drives viral DNA replication and can transform cells.
  • Late region: Encodes structural proteins VP1, VP2, and VP3 (capsid proteins) — expressed after DNA replication begins.
  • Origin of replication (ori): A 64-bp sequence required for viral DNA replication, recognized by T antigen.
  • Transcription regulatory region: Contains promoters and enhancers used in recombinant expression vectors.

Two Infection Outcomes

  • Permissive (lytic) infection: In monkey (COS) cells, SV40 replicates freely, produces progeny virus, and eventually lyses the cell.
  • Non-permissive (abortive) infection: In mouse and human cells lacking T antigen, viral DNA integrates into the host chromosome, transforming the cell but not producing new virus.

Mechanism of Gene Transfer Using SV40 Vector

  1. The late region (coding for capsid proteins) is removed from the SV40 genome and replaced with the foreign gene of interest.
  2. The recombinant SV40 DNA retains the ori and early region (or an artificial promoter).
  3. The recombinant virus is packaged using helper virus or a packaging cell line that provides the capsid proteins in trans.
  4. The packaged recombinant SV40 infects mammalian host cells efficiently, delivering the foreign gene.
  5. Inside the cell, the foreign gene can be expressed transiently (episomal) or stably integrated into the host chromosome.
COS Cell System: COS cells (CV-1 Origin, SV40) are monkey kidney cells stably expressing the SV40 Large T antigen. When recombinant SV40 vectors with ori are introduced into COS cells, they replicate to very high copy numbers — enabling high-level transient gene expression.

Applications

  • Transient and stable expression of recombinant proteins in mammalian cells.
  • Study of gene function, RNA processing, and protein localization in animal cells.
  • SV40 promoter and enhancer sequences are used in many mammalian expression vectors (e.g., pcDNA3.1).
  • Early gene therapy research and vaccine antigen production.

Limitations

  • Insert capacity is small (~2.5 kb — limited by the capsid size ~5.2 kb genome).
  • Requires helper virus or packaging cell line; safety concerns with wild-type SV40 (oncogenic potential).
  • Largely replaced by safer and more efficient viral vectors (retroviruses, adenoviruses, AAV) in modern gene therapy.
Section 5

🔀 Transposons as Vectors

Definition of Transposons

Transposons (transposable elements, or "jumping genes") are mobile DNA sequences that can move from one location to another within a genome — or even between genomes — using a "cut-and-paste" or "copy-and-paste" mechanism. They were discovered by Barbara McClintock in maize in the 1940s (Nobel Prize 1983).

Types of Transposons

  • Class I — Retrotransposons (copy-and-paste mechanism): Transpose via an RNA intermediate; the transposon is first transcribed to RNA, then reverse-transcribed back to DNA, which is inserted at a new site. Examples: Ty elements in yeast, Alu and LINE elements in humans.
  • Class II — DNA Transposons (cut-and-paste mechanism): Encode a transposase enzyme that excises the transposon from its current location and inserts it at a new chromosomal site. Examples: Tn3, Tn10, Tn5 in bacteria; P element in Drosophila; Sleeping Beauty and piggyBac in vertebrates.

Structure of a DNA Transposon

  • Terminal Inverted Repeats (TIRs): Short identical sequences at both ends of the transposon, in inverted orientation. Recognized and bound by transposase.
  • Transposase gene: Encodes the enzyme that catalyzes excision and reintegration.
  • Target Site Duplications (TSDs): Short direct repeats of the host DNA flanking the transposon, created upon insertion.
  • May also carry additional genes such as antibiotic resistance genes (in bacterial transposons).

Mechanism of Transposition (Cut-and-Paste)

  1. Recognition: Transposase enzyme recognizes and binds to the TIRs at both ends of the transposon.
  2. Excision: Transposase cuts both DNA strands at each end of the transposon, excising it from the donor site. A double-strand break is left at the donor locus (usually repaired).
  3. Target capture: The transposase-bound transposon complex finds and binds to a target site in the genome (often AT-rich sequences).
  4. Integration: Transposase makes staggered cuts at the target site and ligates the transposon ends, inserting the transposon into the new location. Host DNA repair fills gaps, creating target site duplications.

Use of Transposons as Vectors in Genetic Engineering

  • Insertional mutagenesis: Transposons are delivered into a genome; random insertion disrupts host genes, creating mutants for phenotypic analysis. Used extensively in Drosophila (P element) and zebrafish.
  • Gene tagging: The transposon acts as a molecular tag; disrupted genes are easily identified by using the transposon sequence as a probe.
  • Stable gene delivery: Engineered transposons (e.g., Sleeping Beauty, piggyBac) carry therapeutic genes and integrate stably into mammalian chromosomes — used in gene therapy research.
  • Transposon delivery systems: The Tn5 transposome system and P element vectors are used for generating transgenic organisms.
  • Next-generation sequencing library preparation: Tn5 transposase is used in ATAC-seq and tagmentation-based library prep protocols.
Sleeping Beauty (SB) Transposon: A reconstructed "ancient" fish transposon, reactivated through molecular evolution. One of the most promising non-viral gene therapy vectors — integrates stably into mammalian genomes with near-random insertion preference (AT-rich TA dinucleotides).

Advantages of Transposons as Vectors

  • No size limitation from viral capsids — can carry larger genes than many viral vectors.
  • Stable genomic integration without viral machinery.
  • Can function in a wide range of host organisms (bacteria to mammals).
  • Simple two-component system: transposon (carrying gene) + transposase (enzyme source).

🎮 Interactive Learning

Hybrid Vector
  • Cosmid = plasmid backbone + lambda cos sites.
  • Developed by Collins and Hohn, 1978.
  • Insert capacity: 35–45 kb.
  • Two cos sites are needed for in vitro phage packaging (requires 37–52 kb total DNA between two cos sites).
  • Replicates like a plasmid inside E. coli; does NOT produce phage particles after entry.
  • Used for constructing genomic libraries and chromosome walking.
Exam tip: Always remember: cos sites enable packaging, ori enables plasmid replication. A cosmid is NOT a phage — it just borrows the phage packaging system for efficient delivery.
Artificial Chromosome
  • Developed by Burke, Carle, and Olson, 1987.
  • Requires: TEL + CEN + ARS + selectable markers.
  • Insert capacity: 200 kb to 2,000 kb (2 Mb) — largest of all vectors.
  • Host: Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baker's yeast).
  • Essential tool in the Human Genome Project.
  • Major limitation: chimeric inserts and instability. BACs later replaced YACs.
Exam tip: CEN = centromere (segregation); ARS = autonomously replicating sequence (origin of replication in yeast); TEL = telomere (end protection). Memorize: CEN-ARS-TEL.
Animal Virus Vector
  • SV40 = Simian Virus 40; discovered 1960.
  • Genome: ~5,243 bp circular dsDNA; non-enveloped icosahedral capsid (45 nm).
  • Early region: Large T antigen (drives DNA replication + cell transformation).
  • Late region: VP1, VP2, VP3 (capsid proteins).
  • Insert capacity: ~2.5 kb (capsid size constraint).
  • COS cells (express Large T antigen) allow episomal amplification of SV40 vectors to very high copy numbers.
  • SV40 ori + promoter/enhancer sequences used in modern mammalian expression vectors.
Exam tip: Two types of SV40 infection: lytic (permissive cells — monkey) and transforming (non-permissive cells — mouse, human). T antigen is the key regulatory protein.
Mobile Genetic Elements
  • Discovered by Barbara McClintock, 1940s (Nobel Prize 1983) in maize (Zea mays).
  • Two classes: Class I (retrotransposons, RNA intermediate) and Class II (DNA transposons, cut-and-paste).
  • Key structural elements: TIRs (terminal inverted repeats) + transposase gene + TSDs.
  • Mechanism: transposase binds TIRs → excises transposon → inserts at new target site.
  • Examples: Tn5, Tn10 (bacteria); P element (Drosophila); Sleeping Beauty, piggyBac (vertebrates).
  • Applications: insertional mutagenesis, gene tagging, stable gene therapy, ATAC-seq library prep (Tn5).
Exam tip: Transposons are NOT plasmids or viruses — they are DNA sequences that move within/between genomes. The key enzyme is transposase. "Jumping genes" is the colloquial term.

Section 12

📖 Glossary of Key Terms

Cosmid
A hybrid cloning vector that combines a plasmid origin of replication and selectable marker with the lambda phage cos sites for in vitro packaging. Accepts 35–45 kb inserts; replicates in E. coli.
cos Site
Twelve-nucleotide single-stranded cohesive (sticky) ends from bacteriophage lambda incorporated into cosmids. Required for in vitro packaging of cosmid DNA into phage heads and efficient delivery into E. coli by transduction.
YAC (Yeast Artificial Chromosome)
An artificially constructed chromosome containing telomeres (TEL), centromere (CEN), origin of replication (ARS), selectable markers, and a cloning site. Accepts 200 kb–2,000 kb inserts in Saccharomyces cerevisiae.
Telomere
Repetitive DNA sequences (TTAGGG in humans) at the ends of linear chromosomes that protect them from degradation and fusion. In YACs, telomeres maintain structural stability and prevent end-to-end chromosome joining.
Centromere
A chromosomal region required for correct segregation of chromosomes during mitosis and meiosis. Binds spindle microtubules via kinetochore proteins. Essential component of YAC for equal distribution to daughter cells.
Transposition
The movement of a transposable element (transposon) from one chromosomal location to another, catalyzed by the transposase enzyme. DNA transposons use a cut-and-paste mechanism; retrotransposons use an RNA intermediate.
Transposase
The enzyme encoded within a DNA transposon that recognizes the terminal inverted repeats (TIRs) and catalyzes excision from the donor site and integration into the new target site. Essential for transposition.
Vector
A DNA molecule (plasmid, phage, artificial chromosome, or mobile element) used as a vehicle to carry a foreign gene into a host cell, where it can be replicated and/or expressed. Must have an origin of replication and selectable marker.
Insert
The foreign DNA fragment (gene of interest) that is cloned into a vector for propagation and/or expression. The insert is ligated into the vector's multiple cloning site (MCS) after restriction enzyme digestion.
Host Cell
The cell into which a recombinant vector is introduced. Provides cellular machinery for vector replication and gene expression. Different vectors require different hosts: bacteria, yeast, mammalian cells.
TIR (Terminal Inverted Repeat)
Short DNA sequences at both ends of a DNA transposon, in opposite (inverted) orientations. Recognized by transposase as the boundaries of the transposon element. Essential for excision and integration during transposition.
Large T Antigen (LT-Ag)
The major SV40 early protein. Functions as a replication initiator by binding to the SV40 ori, and as a transforming protein by inhibiting host tumor suppressors p53 and Rb. Central to SV40 biology and COS cell systems.

  • Transposon: Insert variable (no strict capsid limit, practically ~10–15 kb); host = bacteria to mammals (broad); application = insertional mutagenesis, gene tagging, gene therapy; advantage = stable integration, no viral sequences; limitation = random insertion (genotoxicity risk).
