Gene Cloning

Steps in Cloning  ·  Selection of Clones  ·  Blue-White Screening

How do scientists isolate a single gene from billions of base pairs and make millions of identical copies? Gene cloning is the answer — and it forms the foundation of all recombinant DNA technology, from insulin production to cancer diagnostics.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define gene cloning and explain its importance in animal biotechnology.
  • List all requirements needed for successful gene cloning.
  • Describe each step in the gene cloning process in correct sequence.
  • Explain why clone selection is necessary and list the key selection methods.
  • Describe the blue-white screening system for identifying recombinant clones.
  • Distinguish between transformation, selection, and screening.
Section 1

🔬 Introduction to Gene Cloning

Definition

Gene cloning is the process of making multiple identical copies of a specific gene (or DNA segment) by inserting it into a vector, introducing the recombinant vector into a host cell, and allowing the host to replicate, producing millions of copies of the gene of interest.

Importance in Animal Biotechnology

  • Protein production: Cloned genes can be expressed in bacteria to produce human proteins — insulin, growth hormone, erythropoietin, and clotting factors.
  • Gene therapy: Correct copies of defective genes can be cloned and delivered into patients' cells.
  • Transgenic animals: Cloned genes are introduced into animal embryos to create transgenic models for disease research.
  • Diagnostics: Cloned DNA probes detect pathogens, genetic mutations, and cancer markers.
  • Vaccine development: Antigens encoded by cloned viral genes produce safer subunit vaccines.
Core Concept: Gene cloning = isolate the gene → insert into a vector → replicate in a host cell → select successful recombinants → use for desired purpose.

Basic Concept: How Cloning Copies a Gene

The gene of interest is treated as a molecular "passenger". A vector (commonly a plasmid) acts as the "vehicle". The passenger is loaded into the vehicle using restriction enzymes (cut) and DNA ligase (join). The vehicle enters a bacterial host cell, which divides millions of times — each daughter cell carrying an identical copy of the gene.

Section 2

🧰 Requirements for Gene Cloning

Five essential components must be present for successful gene cloning:

1. Source DNA The DNA containing the gene of interest. Can be genomic DNA (isolated from organism) or cDNA (synthesised from mRNA via reverse transcriptase). This is the "insert" to be cloned.
2. Vector A carrier DNA molecule — usually a plasmid or bacteriophage — that can replicate autonomously in a host cell. Must have: ori, selectable marker, restriction sites.
3. Restriction Enzymes Molecular scissors — endonucleases that recognise specific palindromic sequences and cut both strands of DNA at defined sites, producing compatible sticky or blunt ends on both insert and vector.
4. DNA Ligase Molecular glue — seals the phosphodiester bonds between the insert and linearised vector, joining them into a circular recombinant DNA molecule. T4 DNA ligase is most commonly used.
5. Host Cell Usually Escherichia coli — a competent bacterial cell that takes up the recombinant vector and replicates it during cell division. Each cell propagates one recombinant clone.
Memory Aid: DNA + Vector + Restriction enzymes + Ligase + Host = DVRLH — "Do Very Real Lab Homework!"
Section 3

🪜 Steps in Gene Cloning

Gene cloning proceeds through seven distinct, sequential steps:

1
Isolation of the Gene of Interest

The target gene is obtained either by:

  • Restriction digestion of total genomic DNA — the enzyme cuts DNA and the fragment containing the gene is isolated (e.g., by gel electrophoresis).
  • PCR amplification — if the gene sequence is known, specific primers are used to amplify only that gene.
  • cDNA synthesis — mRNA from a tissue expressing the gene is converted to cDNA using reverse transcriptase.
  • Chemical synthesis — for short, well-characterised genes, the sequence is synthesised de novo.
2
Cutting DNA Using Restriction Enzymes

Both the insert DNA (gene of interest) and the vector DNA are digested with the same restriction enzyme(s). This produces compatible ends:

  • Sticky ends (overhangs) — most common; produced by enzymes like EcoRI, BamHI. Complementary overhangs on insert and vector allow them to anneal by hydrogen bonding.
  • Blunt ends — produced by enzymes like SmaI, EcoRV. No overhang; less efficient for ligation.
Using the same restriction enzyme on both insert and vector guarantees compatible cohesive ends — essential for efficient ligation.
3
Preparation of Vector DNA
  • The vector (e.g., pBR322, pUC19) is linearised by digestion with the same restriction enzyme used on the insert.
  • Optional: the vector may be dephosphorylated using alkaline phosphatase to prevent self-ligation (recircularisation without insert), improving recombinant efficiency.
  • The linearised vector has two free ends with cohesive sequences complementary to the insert ends.
4
Ligation: Joining Insert and Vector

The insert and linearised vector are mixed together. The compatible sticky ends anneal (hydrogen bonds between complementary bases). T4 DNA ligase then seals the phosphodiester bonds between the 3'-OH and 5'-phosphate ends, covalently joining the insert into the vector.

  • The reaction requires: ATP (energy for T4 DNA ligase), Mg²⁺ cofactor, appropriate temperature (16°C overnight for sticky ends).
  • The product is a circular recombinant DNA molecule.
5
Formation of Recombinant DNA

The ligation reaction produces a mixture of molecules:

  • Recombinant vector — vector containing the foreign insert (desired product).
  • Re-ligated vector — vector that recircularised without insert (background).
  • Insert concatemers — multiple inserts ligated together (unwanted).

The recombinant DNA molecule is now ready for introduction into the host cell. This is the central product of the cloning process.

6
Transformation into Host Cell

The recombinant DNA is introduced into competent E. coli cells by transformation:

  • Chemical transformation: Competent cells are prepared by treating with CaCl₂ (disrupts cell membrane). Recombinant DNA is added and the mixture is briefly heat-shocked at 42°C, causing the DNA to enter the cells.
  • Electroporation: A brief high-voltage electric pulse creates temporary pores in the cell membrane, allowing DNA entry. Higher efficiency than chemical transformation.
Not all cells take up the vector — typically only 1 in 10³–10⁵ cells is successfully transformed. Selection is therefore essential.
7
Multiplication of Clones

Transformed bacteria are plated on selective agar medium (e.g., containing antibiotic). Each transformed cell that survives and divides produces a colony of genetically identical cells — a clone.

  • Each colony contains millions of copies of the recombinant vector (and hence the cloned gene).
  • The colonies are then screened to identify those carrying the correct insert.
  • Selected colonies are grown in liquid culture for large-scale DNA/protein production.

Section 4

🎯 Selection of Clones

Why Is Selection Necessary?

After transformation, only a small fraction of bacterial cells actually take up the recombinant vector. The transformation mixture contains:

  • Non-transformed cells — did not take up any vector (majority).
  • Cells with re-ligated vector — took up vector that re-circularised without insert.
  • Cells with recombinant vector — took up vector containing the gene of interest (desired).
The Problem: All three types of cells look identical on a plate. Without selection and screening, finding the correct recombinant clone would be like finding a needle in a haystack of billions of bacteria.

Method 1 — Antibiotic Resistance Markers

Principle: Vectors carry antibiotic resistance genes (e.g., ampr for ampicillin resistance in pBR322). Only cells that have successfully taken up the vector will carry this resistance gene and survive on antibiotic-containing agar.

  • Transformed cells are plated on medium containing ampicillin.
  • Only cells that took up the vector (carrying ampr) grow → they are transformants.
  • Non-transformed cells die — they cannot replicate on ampicillin medium.
  • This step identifies transformed vs non-transformed cells, but does NOT yet distinguish recombinant from non-recombinant transformants.

Method 2 — Insertional Inactivation

Using pBR322 as an example: the vector carries both ampr and tetr (tetracycline resistance) genes. When the gene of interest is cloned into a restriction site within the tetr gene, the tetracycline resistance is disrupted.

  • Non-recombinant transformants: ampicillin-resistant AND tetracycline-resistant (tetr gene intact).
  • Recombinant transformants: ampicillin-resistant but tetracycline-sensitive (tetr gene disrupted by insert).
  • Grow colonies on ampicillin first → replica plate onto tetracycline → colonies that grow on amp but NOT on tet are recombinants.

Method 3 — Blue-White Screening (lacZ System)

Used with vectors like pUC19 that contain the lacZ gene (encoding β-galactosidase) with an MCS (multiple cloning site) within the lacZ coding sequence.

  • When no insert is present, the lacZ gene is intact → β-galactosidase is produced → cleaves X-gal (a colourless substrate) → produces a blue compound → colony appears blue.
  • When an insert disrupts the MCS within lacZ, β-galactosidase is non-functional → X-gal is not cleaved → colony remains white.
  • Blue colonies = non-recombinant (no insert, intact lacZ).
  • White colonies = recombinant (insert present, disrupted lacZ).
Exam Formula: Blue = Bad (no insert, lacZ active) | White = Winner (has insert, lacZ disrupted). Pick the white colonies!

Method 4 — Probe-Based Screening (Colony Hybridisation)

Once transformants are identified by selection, the correct clone carrying the gene of interest is found by hybridisation:

  • Bacterial colonies are transferred to a nylon membrane and lysed.
  • DNA is denatured and fixed to the membrane.
  • A labelled DNA probe (complementary to the gene of interest) is applied.
  • Probe hybridises to colonies carrying the matching DNA sequence.
  • Positive colonies are identified by autoradiography or fluorescence.

Section 8 — Interactive

🎮 Interactive Learning

Key Concept

Using the same restriction enzyme on both insert and vector ensures they both have complementary cohesive (sticky) ends. For example, if EcoRI is used on both, the insert ends with 5'-AATT overhangs and the vector ends with 5'-AATT overhangs — these can anneal by hydrogen bonding before DNA ligase seals them.

If different enzymes are used, the ends would be incompatible — they cannot anneal, so ligation efficiency drops to near zero.

Exception: Blunt-end ligation — any blunt-ended insert can be ligated into any blunt-ended vector (e.g., using SmaI), but this is far less efficient and non-directional.
Transformation

Bacterial cells are not normally able to take up naked DNA from their environment. They must be made "competent" — artificially rendered capable of DNA uptake.

  • Chemical method (CaCl₂): Bacteria are washed in ice-cold CaCl₂ solution. The calcium ions partially disrupt the cell membrane and create a state where DNA can adsorb to the surface. Heat shock (42°C for 90 seconds) drives the DNA into the cell.
  • Electroporation: A high-voltage pulse (1,500–2,500 V/cm) creates transient pores in the membrane. More efficient, used for difficult-to-transform bacteria.
Clone Selection

When a vector is linearised by a restriction enzyme, the two ends have 5'-phosphate groups. These can re-ligate (vector re-circularises without insert), increasing the background of non-recombinant colonies.

Alkaline phosphatase (CIAP or SAP) removes the 5'-phosphate groups from the linearised vector. Without phosphates, the vector ends cannot self-ligate — only when insert DNA (which retains its 5'-phosphates) is added can ligation occur. This greatly reduces background and increases recombinant efficiency.

Exam Prep

The correct sequence for gene cloning (must memorise!):

  1. Isolate gene of interest (restriction digestion / PCR / cDNA)
  2. Cut both insert and vector with same restriction enzyme → compatible sticky ends
  3. Prepare vector (linearise; optionally dephosphorylate with alkaline phosphatase)
  4. Ligate insert + vector using T4 DNA ligase → recombinant DNA
  5. Transform recombinant DNA into competent E. coli (heat shock or electroporation)
  6. Plate on selective medium (ampicillin) → only transformed cells grow
  7. Screen for recombinants (blue-white / insertional inactivation)
  8. Confirm correct clone (restriction mapping / PCR / sequencing)
  9. Amplify and use (large-scale growth, protein expression, etc.)

📖 Glossary

Gene Cloning
The process of making multiple identical copies of a specific gene by inserting it into a vector, introducing the recombinant vector into a host cell, and propagating the host through cell division.
Vector
A DNA molecule (plasmid, phage, cosmid) used to carry a foreign gene into a host cell. Must have an origin of replication, selectable marker, and restriction sites. Acts as the "vehicle" for gene delivery.
Ligation
The covalent joining of two DNA molecules by DNA ligase. Forms phosphodiester bonds between the 3'-OH and 5'-phosphate termini of compatible DNA ends. Creates the recombinant DNA molecule in gene cloning.
Transformation
The uptake of foreign DNA by a bacterial cell. In gene cloning, competent E. coli cells take up recombinant vectors by heat shock (CaCl₂ method) or electroporation.
Recombinant DNA
A hybrid DNA molecule formed by joining DNA sequences from two different sources — specifically the gene of interest (insert) ligated into a cloning vector. The central product of the cloning process.
Selection Marker
A gene on the vector (usually an antibiotic resistance gene) used to identify and select transformed host cells. Only cells carrying the vector survive on medium with the corresponding antibiotic.
Blue-White Screening
A method to distinguish recombinant from non-recombinant clones. White colonies carry an insert (lacZ disrupted, no β-gal, X-gal not cleaved). Blue colonies have no insert (lacZ intact, β-gal cleaves X-gal → blue colour).
Insertional Inactivation
Disruption of a marker gene (e.g., tet^r) by inserting foreign DNA into a restriction site within it. Cells with the insert lose function of that marker. Used to distinguish recombinant from non-recombinant clones.
Competent Cell
A bacterial cell that has been chemically (CaCl₂) or physically (electroporation) treated to become capable of taking up exogenous DNA from its environment during transformation.
Sticky Ends
Short single-stranded complementary overhangs produced by staggered restriction enzyme cuts. Compatible sticky ends on the insert and vector anneal by hydrogen bonding, facilitating efficient ligation.
X-gal
5-Bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl-β-D-galactopyranoside — a synthetic colourless substrate for β-galactosidase. When cleaved by β-gal, it produces a blue compound. Used in blue-white screening with IPTG inducer.
Colony Hybridisation
A probe-based screening method. Bacterial colonies are lysed on a nylon membrane, DNA denatured, and a labelled probe applied. Hybridisation reveals which colony contains the target gene.
