Transgenic Animal Technology

Transgenesis, Knock-out Mice, Dolly and Animal Bioreactors

Adding, removing or editing a gene in an animal is one of the most powerful ways to learn what genes do and to make useful products. This module covers the concept of transgenesis, how transgenic and knock-out animals are made, the cloning of Dolly, and the use of animals as bioreactors.

📗 Chapter 1

Transgenesis: Concept and Transgenes

A transgenic animal carries a foreign gene in every cell and passes it to its offspring.

Transgenic animal
An animal whose genome has been altered by the stable introduction of a foreign gene (transgene) into its germ line, so that the gene is inherited by the next generation.
Key terms
TermMeaning
TransgeneThe foreign gene (or modified gene) introduced into the animal
TransgenesisThe process of producing a transgenic organism
Founder animalThe first transgenic animal carrying the transgene in its germ line
Germline transmissionPassing of the transgene through gametes to offspring
Knock-outA gene made non-functional (loss of function)
Knock-inA gene replaced or inserted at a defined locus (gain/alteration of function)
Structure of a transgene construct
Fig. 1. A typical transgene: promoter + coding sequence + polyadenylation signal (+ selectable marker).
Why make transgenic animals?
💊

Medicine

Produce human therapeutic proteins in milk or blood.

🧬

Research

Study gene function and model human diseases (e.g. cancer, cystic fibrosis).

🌾

Agriculture

Faster growth, disease resistance, improved milk or wool.

💪

Testing

Test vaccine and drug safety in animals with human-like responses.

Landmark: the "super mouse" (1982)

Palmiter and Brinster injected the rat growth hormone gene fused to the mouse metallothionein promoter into mouse eggs. Transgenic mice grew almost twice the size of normal littermates.

2. Embryonic stem (ES) cell-mediated transfer
  • ES cells are pluripotent cells from the inner cell mass of a blastocyst (Evans & Kaufman, 1981).
  • DNA is introduced into cultured ES cells; correct clones are selected in vitro.
  • Selected ES cells are injected into a host blastocyst to give a chimeric animal.
  • Allows targeted changes by homologous recombination — the basis for knock-outs.

📙 Chapter 3

Transgenic Animal Models: Knock-out Mice

Knock-out mice have a chosen gene switched off, revealing what the gene normally does.

Gene knock-out
The deliberate inactivation of a specific gene by gene targeting: homologous recombination replaces or disrupts the gene in embryonic stem cells, from which a mutant mouse is derived.
Capecchi, Evans and Smithies received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2007 for this work
Fig. 2. Production of a knock-out mouse by gene targeting in ES cells.
Positive-negative selection
MarkerPosition in vectorDrugOutcome
neoRInside the homologous region (disrupts the target gene)G418 (neomycin analogue)Only cells that took up the vector survive (positive selection)
tk (HSV thymidine kinase)Outside the homology armsGanciclovirCells with random integration keep tk and die (negative selection)
ResultSurvivors are enriched for correct homologous recombination
🎨
Why use coat colour?▼
ES cells from the 129 strain carry the agouti coat colour allele; host blastocysts from C57BL/6 are black. Agouti patches in a chimera show that ES-derived cells contribute, and agouti offspring after mating show germline transmission.
✂
Conditional knock-outs (Cre-lox)▼
If a gene is essential for development, complete knock-out is lethal. The Cre recombinase / loxP system deletes the gene only in chosen tissues or at a chosen time.
💪
Knock-out vs knock-in▼
Knock-out: gene function removed. Knock-in: a gene or mutation is inserted at a specific locus (e.g. a human disease allele).
🔮
Examples▼
  • CFTR knock-out mouse: model for cystic fibrosis.
  • p53 knock-out mouse: develops tumours early — model for cancer.
  • Oncomouse (Harvard, 1988): first patented animal; carries an activated oncogene.

Dolly the Sheep: Cloning by SCNT

Dolly was the first mammal cloned from an adult somatic cell, proving that differentiated cells can be reprogrammed.

Dolly was produced by Ian Wilmut, Keith Campbell and colleagues at the Roslin Institute, Edinburgh. The result was published in Nature in 1997. She was named after Dolly Parton, because the donor cell came from a mammary gland.

Fig. 3. Somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT) used to clone Dolly. The lamb was white-faced like the Finn Dorset donor, proving the nucleus came from the donor.

What Dolly proved

  • The nucleus of a differentiated adult cell still contains the complete genome.
  • The egg cytoplasm can reprogram it to totipotency.
  • Led to induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs, 2006).

Facts that examiners ask

  • Of 277 reconstructed embryos, 29 developed to blastocysts, 13 ewes were implanted and only one lamb (Dolly) was born.
  • Dolly gave birth to six lambs by natural mating.
  • She was euthanised in February 2003 because of progressive lung disease; she also had arthritis. Her telomeres were somewhat shorter, debated as a sign of premature ageing.

Concerns about cloning

  • Very low success rate and abnormal pregnancies/large offspring.
  • Animal welfare concerns.
  • Ethical objections to human reproductive cloning, which is prohibited in many countries (including guidelines in India).

Applications: Animals as Bioreactors

Transgenic animals can act as living factories (pharming) that secrete valuable human proteins in milk, blood or eggs.

Pharming (pharmaceutical farming) places a human gene under a mammary-specific promoter, such as β-lactoglobulin, β-casein or whey acidic protein. The protein is made only in the mammary gland and secreted into milk, from which it is purified.

Other applications of transgenic animals

  • Disease models: cancer, Alzheimer's, diabetes.
  • Vaccine and drug safety testing.
  • Agriculture: AquAdvantage salmon (faster growth, approved by FDA in 2015).
  • Biological research tools: GFP-expressing animals to track cells.

Ethical and safety issues

  • Animal welfare and the 3Rs (Replace, Reduce, Refine).
  • Possible gene flow to wild populations.
  • Patenting of animals; public acceptance.

Short-answer questions

Click a question to reveal the model answer.

Q.1Define transgenic animal. Give two examples.▼

A transgenic animal carries a foreign gene (transgene) stably integrated in its genome and transmitted to offspring. Examples: super mouse with rat growth hormone gene; goat producing human antithrombin in milk.

Q.2Describe pronuclear microinjection.▼

Fertilised eggs are collected from superovulated females; linearised transgene DNA is injected into the male pronucleus; embryos are transferred into pseudopregnant foster mothers; pups are screened by PCR or Southern blotting of tail DNA; founders are bred to establish a line.

Q.3How is a knock-out mouse produced?▼

A targeting vector containing neoR (inside the homology region) and tk (outside) is introduced into ES cells; positive-negative selection (G418 + ganciclovir) enriches homologous recombinants; selected ES cells are injected into blastocysts to give chimeras; germline transmission yields heterozygotes, which are inter-crossed to obtain homozygous knock-outs.

Q.4Explain how Dolly was created.▼

A nucleus from a mammary gland cell of a Finn Dorset ewe was fused by electric pulse with an enucleated egg of a Scottish Blackface ewe; the embryo was cultured and implanted into a surrogate ewe. Dolly was born on 5 July 1996 at the Roslin Institute.

Q.5What is meant by animals as bioreactors?▼

Transgenic animals that produce recombinant human proteins in milk or other body fluids under tissue-specific promoters, e.g. antithrombin in goat milk and alpha-1-antitrypsin in sheep milk.

Q.6Mention two ethical issues of transgenic animals.▼

Animal suffering and welfare; ecological risk and gene flow; patenting of life forms.

📖 Glossary

Key Terms & Glossary

Important terms in simple language.

Transgene
A foreign gene introduced into an organism.
Founder
The first transgenic animal of a line.
Pseudopregnant female
Female mated with a vasectomised male; her uterus is ready to receive embryos.
ES cell
Embryonic stem cell: pluripotent cell from the blastocyst inner cell mass.
Chimera
An animal made of cells from two genetically different embryos.
Homologous recombination
Exchange between identical DNA sequences, used for gene targeting.
SCNT
Somatic cell nuclear transfer: transfer of a somatic nucleus into an enucleated egg.
Knock-out
Loss-of-function mutation created in a chosen gene.
Pharming
Production of pharmaceutical proteins in farm animals or plants.
Cre-lox
Recombinase system for conditional (tissue-specific) gene deletion.
