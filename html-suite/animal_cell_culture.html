Unit Overview

Animal Cell Culture

Animal cell culture is the process of growing cells isolated from animal tissues under controlled in vitro conditions — temperature, pH, CO₂, and nutrition. It is a cornerstone technique enabling research in cancer biology, drug development, vaccine production, and regenerative medicine.

🔬 Annotated Animal Cell — Key Organelles

🎯

Definition

Removal of cells from an animal and growing them under controlled in vitro conditions — mimicking the in vivo environment of temperature, pH, CO₂, osmolarity, and nutrients.

📜

Historical Milestones

  • 1907 — Ross Harrison: nerve fiber culture
  • 1943 — Earle: mouse fibroblast monolayer
  • 1951 — HeLa cells (first immortal line)
  • 1975 — Monoclonal antibody technology
  • 1998 — Human ES cell isolation
  • 2006 — iPSC generation (Yamanaka)
💡

Applications

  • Vaccine & biopharmaceutical production
  • Cancer biology research
  • Drug toxicity & screening
  • Genetic engineering
  • Tissue engineering & regenerative medicine
  • Virology studies
⚠️

Limitations

  • Loss of 3D cell–cell interactions
  • Genetic drift over passages
  • Risk of mycoplasma contamination
  • Expensive and labor-intensive
  • May not replicate in vivo physiology fully

🌡️

Temperature & Atmosphere

  • Mammalian cells: 37°C
  • CO₂ incubator: 5–10% CO₂
  • CO₂ buffers bicarbonate in media
  • Humidity: ≥95% prevents evaporation
  • Bird-derived cells: 38.5°C
⚗️

pH & Osmolarity

  • Optimal pH: 7.2 – 7.4
  • Indicator: Phenol red (yellow = acidic)
  • Buffering: NaHCO₃ / HEPES
  • Osmolarity: 280–320 mOsm/kg
  • NaCl adjusts osmotic pressure
🧲

Substrata for Cell Attachment

  • Tissue culture polystyrene (TCPS)
  • Fibronectin / collagen coating
  • Poly-L-lysine for neurons
  • Matrigel for 3D culture
  • Suspension culture for non-adherent cells

Module 3

Primary Culture

A primary culture is established directly from excised tissue or organs. Cells are dissociated and cultured in vitro for the first time. Primary cultures are finite — they can only be maintained for a limited number of passages before senescence occurs (Hayflick limit).

🔄 Primary Culture — Step-by-Step Process Flow

Animal Tissue CollectionSterile biopsy / organ harvest
▼
Wash with PBS / Balanced Salt SolutionRemove blood, debris, bacteria
▼
Mechanical DissociationMincing, sieving, scraping
OR
Enzymatic DissociationTrypsin / Collagenase / Dispase
▼
Single Cell SuspensionFilter through 70–100 µm cell strainer
▼
Centrifuge 200 × g, 5 min → Discard Supernatant
▼
Resuspend in Complete Culture MediumDMEM + 10% FBS + Pen-Strep
▼
Hemocytometer Count + Trypan Blue Viability ≥ 90%
▼
Seed in Culture Flask — T25 / T75 / 6-well plate
▼
Incubate: 37°C · 5% CO₂ · 95% HumidityCheck 24–48 hrs for attachment & morphology
▼
Adherent CultureFibroblasts, epithelial cells
Suspension CultureLymphocytes, hematopoietic cells
1

Tissue Preparation

Tissue collected aseptically, placed in cold PBS/HBSS, and minced into 1–2 mm³ fragments. Blood and connective tissue removed by repeated washing.

2

Enzymatic Dissociation

Trypsin (0.05–0.25%) cleaves surface proteins disrupting cell–cell contacts. Collagenase (Type I–IV) digests ECM collagen. Incubate at 37°C for 15–60 min with gentle agitation.

3

Neutralization & Centrifugation

FBS neutralizes trypsin activity. Centrifuge at 200×g for 5 min. Discard supernatant, resuspend pellet in complete medium.

4

Cell Counting & Viability

Count using hemocytometer. Trypan blue exclusion: live cells remain clear, dead cells stain blue. Viability should be ≥90% for optimal culture establishment.

5

Plating & Monitoring

Seed at 10⁴–10⁵ cells/cm². Incubate in CO₂ incubator. Monitor daily by inverted microscopy for morphology, attachment, and confluence.

⚠️ Explant vs. Monolayer Culture

Explant culture: Small tissue fragments placed directly on surface; cells migrate outward. Preserves tissue architecture briefly.

Monolayer culture: Dissociated single cells attach and grow flat. More reproducible and amenable to quantitative analysis.

📉 Limitations of Primary Culture

  • Finite lifespan — Hayflick limit (~50 divisions)
  • Heterogeneous cell population
  • Batch variability between preparations
  • Requires fresh tissue each time
  • Cells undergo crisis and eventual senescence
Module 4

Cell Lines

When a primary culture is subcultured (passaged) for the first time, it becomes a cell line. Cell lines may be finite (limited passages) or continuous/immortalized (indefinitely proliferating). They are essential tools in biological research and industrial biomanufacturing.

🔁 Subculturing (Passaging) — Process Flow

Confluent Monolayer Culture (~80–90% confluency)
▼
Remove Medium → Wash with PBS (removes serum)
▼
Add Trypsin-EDTA (0.05%) · Incubate 3–5 min at 37°C
▼
Neutralize with Complete Medium (3× volume with FBS)
▼
Centrifuge · Count · Resuspend · Trypan Blue Viability Check
▼
Seed into New Flask at 1:3 – 1:10 Split Ratio
▼
Continue PassagingFinite: ~20–50 passages
ImmortalizationSpontaneous / viral / oncogene
Cell BankingCryopreservation at −196°C

Module 5

Stem Cells: Types, Culture & Applications

Stem cells are undifferentiated cells capable of self-renewal and differentiation into specialized cell types. They hold immense therapeutic potential in regenerative medicine, disease modeling, and drug discovery.

🔺 Stem Cell Potency Hierarchy

🥚

Embryonic Stem Cells (ESCs)

Derived from the inner cell mass of blastocyst (3–5 days). Pluripotent — form all 3 germ layers. Cultured on feeder layers (MEFs) or Matrigel with LIF/bFGF.

🔄

Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells (iPSCs)

Adult somatic cells reprogrammed using Yamanaka factors: Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, c-Myc (2006 Nobel). Avoids ESC ethical issues. Enables autologous therapy.

🩸

Hematopoietic Stem Cells (HSCs)

Found in bone marrow (Lin⁻ Sca1⁺ c-Kit⁺). Give rise to all blood cell lineages. Used clinically in bone marrow transplantation. CD34⁺ in humans.

💪

Mesenchymal Stem Cells (MSCs)

Stromal cells from bone marrow, adipose, umbilical cord. Differentiate into osteoblasts, chondrocytes, adipocytes. CD105⁺ CD73⁺ CD90⁺ surface markers.

🧠

Neural Stem Cells (NSCs)

Reside in subventricular zone and hippocampus. Form neurospheres in vitro. Differentiate into neurons, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes.

🫀

Cardiac Stem Cells

Found in cardiac niches (c-Kit⁺ or Sca1⁺). Can regenerate cardiomyocytes post-myocardial infarction. Key target of cardiac regenerative therapy.

Module 6

Organ Culture

Organ culture maintains the three-dimensional architecture and tissue organization of whole organs or organ fragments in vitro. Unlike cell culture, it preserves cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions, providing a more physiologically relevant experimental model.

📊 Methods of Organ Culture — Flow Classification

Organ Culture
▼
Watch Glass Method
Organ on plasma clot in watch glass. Early method by Fell & Robison (1929)
Grid / Raft Method
Organ on stainless steel grid at liquid–air interface; widely used for skin, limb buds
Perfusion Culture
Oxygenated medium perfused through intact organ vasculature continuously
Roller Tube Method
Slowly rotating tube ensures even medium distribution around organ slices

✅ Advantages of Organ Culture

  • Preserves 3D tissue architecture & histology
  • Maintains cell–cell & cell–ECM interactions
  • Closer to in vivo physiology than cell culture
  • Suitable for short-term drug testing
  • Ideal for developmental biology studies

🔬 Applications of Organ Culture

  • Limb bud & developmental biology studies
  • Cornea, thyroid, pancreas organ cultures
  • Testing teratogenic compounds
  • Drug metabolism studies (liver slices)
  • Tumor microenvironment research

Artificial Skin — Applied Organ Culture

Artificial skin (tissue-engineered skin) is a bioengineered construct recreating the epidermal-dermal interface using keratinocytes and fibroblasts on a scaffold. It is a landmark application of organ culture technology for treating severe burns and chronic wounds.

✅ Clinical Advantages

  • Overcomes donor site shortage in severe burns
  • Autologous constructs — no immune rejection
  • Reduced scarring vs. split-thickness grafts
  • Off-the-shelf allogeneic products available
  • Platform for cosmetic drug testing (EpiDerm™)
  • Alternative to animal testing

🔭 Future Challenges

  • Vascularization — no capillary network
  • Lack of hair follicles, sweat/sebaceous glands
  • Melanocyte integration for skin pigmentation
  • Innervation — no sensory nerve endings
  • Long-term mechanical durability
  • High manufacturing cost

🖨️ 3D Bioprinting of Skin — Emerging Frontier

3D bioprinting deposits cell-laden bioinks (keratinocytes, fibroblasts, melanocytes) layer-by-layer with high precision. Recent advances achieve full-thickness skin substitutes with melanocyte integration. Companies like CELLINK and Organovo are pioneering commercial bioprinted skin platforms for wound healing therapies and cosmetic safety testing, moving toward in situ printing directly on wounds.
